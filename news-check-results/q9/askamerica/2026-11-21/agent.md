## Central claim: true
"An estimated 75% of Michigan's rivers and streams likely exceed state limits for fecal contamination" (Molly Rippke, EGLE). It is reported faithfully as a preliminary agency estimate, and the article hedges it (most waters are not routinely sampled). Warehouse check (environment.water_quality_samples, Michigan E. coli, 2021-2025, 1,493 sites): 78% had at least one sample over 300/100 mL, 55% had one over 1,000, and 68% had a mean over 130. This is a single-sample proxy on non-random sites, not the legal geometric-mean test, so it supports direction and not the exact 75%.

## Other claims
- Michigan is the 10th most populous state: true. census.pep_population 2023 shows 10,037,261, behind CA, TX, FL, NY, PA, IL, OH, GA and NC.
- "Vast majority exceed swimming levels; over half often exceed partial-contact levels": true, consistent with the warehouse proxy above.
- State standards (300 at any time, 130 over 30 days, 1,000 partial-contact): mostly true. EGLE defines 300 and 1,000 as daily maximum geometric means, and the swimming limits apply May 1 to Oct 31. The article's "at any given time" simplifies this.
- CDC "could top a million": mostly true, but a soft understatement. The linked CDC EID paper (2014 data) estimates 5.61 million recreational-water illnesses (95% CrI 2.97-9.00 million), 1.13 million from drinking water, and 7.15 million waterborne illnesses in total. Most recreational cases are ear infections, not gut illness.
- $13,000 home-price effect: true. The Cambridge study reports an 8.9% drop (over $13,000 for the average house) for sales during outbreaks, and says the effect does not persist.
- 76,000 river miles: true as far as I could check. It is repeated in Bridge's septic story, and I did not find an agency page for it.
- Not checkable: Rep. McFall's quote about industries' "own self-interests" is a figurative opinion about motives.
- Unsupported (no independent source found): Indiana 74% and Illinois 83% of assessed river miles; Environment America 69% across 17 states (page returned HTTP 403); "one full-time employee" monitoring river E. coli; $40,000 annual operating cash per conservation district. The only copy I found, Michigan Public, is a verbatim republication of the Bridge article, so it is not independent evidence. Other cited claims (Grand River 252 miles, 12.4M river users and 30.7M beach swimmers, "billions spent" on cleanup) were not graded or verified.

## Framing flags (not enough for a Pinocchio)
- The headline ("3 of 4 rivers") drops the body's hedges: it is an estimate from partial, non-random sampling, covers rivers and streams, and the article says waters are not always polluted.
- EGLE's own E. coli page still says roughly half of rivers exceed the swimming standard. The article does not reconcile this with 75%.
- The CDC citation understates the source's own estimate.
- The Reclaim the Rivers page lists an $89 million commitment. The article's "billions" was not verified.

Warehouse tables used: environment.water_quality_samples and census.pep_population. EGLE, the CDC EID paper and the Cambridge study were fetched as primary sources. The Indiana and Illinois figures and the Environment America study could not be checked against their own documents.
