The Gazette article's claims about Colorado's 2026 drought, Colorado Springs water storage and water-use conservation are somewhat biased and partly false; 3 claims could not be confirmed.

**Rating: 2 Pinocchios.**

Article: guest opinion by Travas Deal, CEO of Colorado Springs Utilities (Gazette, 2026-11-17). It is advocacy by the utility's own chief, so every figure is self-reported.

**Claim results**
- True: the winter was the warmest on record. The Colorado Climate Center (CCC) says the first half of water year 2026 was the warmest on record, 3F above any prior October-March, and March was the warmest in 132 years.
- True: snowpack was 22% of normal. CCC reports 22% of the 1991-2020 median on April 1, the lowest on record.
- True: drought across the state. Warehouse weather.drought_monitor_weekly for El Paso County, weeks from 2026-09-01 to 2026-11-10, shows an average of 92% of area in D1 or worse and 79% in D2 or worse.
- True: three years of demand in storage at the start of 2026. KRDO in March reported reservoirs 77% full, about three years of demand per CSU. That is a March figure, not January.
- Mostly false (central claim): "Since 2000, total water use has been reduced by nearly half, even as our city grew by 42%." KUNC, quoting CSU's conservation manager, reports that from 2000 to 2023 total deliveries fell about 25% and per-capita use fell 39%, while the city grew about 40%. The warehouse (geo.gazetteer_places) puts Colorado Springs at 487,887 in 2024, about +35% on the 2000 census count of 360,890 (the 2000 count is from memory, not fetched). "Nearly half" fits neither total nor per-capita use. The 42% growth figure is roughly plausible, perhaps on a service-area basis.
- Unsupported: CSU had 40% of its typical water yield. A search summary of CSU reporting says about 41% expected, and KRDO gave 54% of runoff in March. No primary CSU page loaded, so I could not confirm it.
- Unsupported: about 70% of supply comes from the Colorado River Basin. The CPR and army.mil pages returned HTTP 403, and nothing else stated the share.
- Unsupported: 58% of average precipitation year to date. I did not run a Pikes Peak normal comparison and found no outside page with the figure.
- Not verified: more than two years of storage entering 2027, and state climatologists' forecast of average snowpack at best.

**Misleading framing**
- Mixing per-person and total use makes the conservation record look about twice as large as the 25% cut in total deliveries.
- "Water continued flowing" is credited to planning and conservation. The piece underplays that most of the buffer was reservoir storage, which the article itself says dropped from about three years of demand to a little over two.

**Method notes**
- The warehouse has no snowpack or utility-supply tables (search_catalog: "snowpack" and "snotel" matched nothing). Those claims were checked against CCC and KRDO instead.
- The 2000 baseline for the growth figure is not in the warehouse and was not fetched.
