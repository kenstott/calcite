# Fact-Check: CCJ Mid-Year 2026 Homicide Trends Report

## Claim 1: "The average reported homicide rate in the first half of 2026 was 18% lower than during the same period in 2025"

**Verdict:** TRUE — accurately quoted from the primary source.

**Evidence:** CCJ's "Crime Trends in U.S. Cities: Mid-Year 2026 Update" (counciloncj.org, published July 23, 2026) reports homicide down 18% in the first half of 2026 vs. first half of 2025, representing 215 fewer homicides among the cities reporting homicide data. Companion coverage (UPI, Stateline, The Hill, JURIST) all corroborate the 18% figure and attribute it to CCJ directly. Separately, the FBI's own preliminary quarterly UCR data (per Stateline, Aug 2026 coverage) shows murder/nonnegligent manslaughter down an even larger 23% for the comparable 2026-vs-2025 period nationally — a different, non-overlapping data source (all reporting agencies vs. CCJ's city sample) that corroborates the direction and magnitude (in fact shows a *larger* decline), which argues against the CCJ figure being an outlier or cherry-picked.

**Confidence:** High — directly confirmed against CCJ's own published report and independently corroborated by FBI preliminary data.

## Claim 2: "51% decline from the 2022 peak"

**Verdict:** TRUE — accurately quoted from the primary source.

**Evidence:** The same CCJ mid-year 2026 report states first-half-2026 homicide was 51% lower than first-half-2022 (the post-pandemic peak year for U.S. homicide). This is consistent with the well-documented trajectory: 2022 was a local peak, followed by four consecutive years of large declines (2023, 2024, 2025, and now 2026), a pattern independently tracked by Jeff Asher's analyses ("largest one-year drop in murder ever recorded" repeated in 2023, 2024, and 2025 per multiple outlets).

**Confidence:** High — directly confirmed against CCJ's own report; consistent with independently reported multi-year trend data.

## Claim 3: "Predicts 2026's homicide rate will likely dip further, again reaching a new historic low... potentially the lowest level since at least 1900 (hedged as conditional)"

**Verdict:** TRUE as characterized — the hedging is real and accurately represented.

**Evidence:** CCJ's mid-year 2026 report itself uses "will likely dip further, again reaching a new historic low" — conditional language, not an assertion of fact. The "lowest since at least 1900" framing originates from CCJ's *prior* report (on full-year 2025 data), which projected 2025's rate would likely be the lowest since at least 1900 pending final FBI numbers — CCJ is explicit that this rests on preliminary data and is not yet confirmed. Multiple outlets (CBS News, Baltimore Sun, JURIST) independently report FBI preliminary 2025 data putting the U.S. murder rate at "the lowest level on record" / "20-year low" territory, and separately Jeff Asher has predicted the 2026 Crime in the Nation report will show the fewest murders since 1967 and a rate below 4/100k — "easily the lowest ever recorded." So the hedge is consistently present across CCJ's own materials and is not overstated by the claim as presented.

**Historical-comparison scrutiny — "lowest since at least 1900":** This is a genuinely fragile comparison on data-quality grounds, though CCJ's own hedging (using "at least 1900" rather than a firm claim, and "likely") appropriately reflects that fragility rather than ignoring it. National U.S. mortality/homicide data before FBI UCR began (1930) relies on the Death Registration Area (DRA), which:
- Covered only a handful of states in 1900 and did not reach full national coverage (all states + territories) until 1933.
- Is documented in criminological/demographic literature (e.g., Springer *Demography* econometric-forecasting work on early-20th-century U.S. homicide rates, and CDC/NCHS vital statistics history) as having systematically undercounted homicides in its first decade — deaths were often misclassified as "accidents" due to insufficient reporting by local authorities, and the states in the DRA disproportionately were lower-homicide states, biasing early rates downward and making the apparent rise from 1900–1933 potentially illusory (an artifact of expanding, improving coverage rather than a real trend).
- Therefore "lowest since at least 1900" is a claim built on genuinely weak comparison-year data (pre-1933 rates are estimates with substantial uncertainty), not verified national statistics comparable in quality to modern UCR/NVSS data. It is a defensible *statement of best available evidence* given the "at least" qualifier and repeated hedging, but would be overstated if presented without that hedge — recent researchers who reconstruct 1900-era rates via econometric methods generally still conclude modern rates are historically very low, so the qualitative claim is likely directionally correct, but the precision implied by "since 1900" is not supportable to more than order-of-magnitude confidence.

**Confidence:** Medium — the hedged framing is accurately represented and the qualitative "historic low" claim is well supported by modern data (FBI/UCR + independent RTCI), but the specific "since 1900" comparison rests on early-20th-century data of documented poor quality, so precision beyond "lowest in the modern UCR era" (post-1930s) cannot be verified to high confidence.

## Claim 4: Sample basis — 30-36 cities as grounds for a national claim

**Verdict:** MOSTLY TRUE / needs context — the sample is a convenience sample, not a probability sample, but CCJ, independent journalists, and FBI's own broader preliminary data all point the same direction, which meaningfully mitigates (but doesn't eliminate) the representativeness concern.

**Evidence:** CCJ's near-real-time crime trends reports (running since July 2020) explicitly draw on "a limited number of cities across the nation" — i.e., a convenience sample of large cities willing/able to share timely data, not a randomly or proportionally selected national sample. This is a real methodological limitation: large cities are not representative of small/mid-size cities, suburbs, or rural areas, and city participation is not random (cities with worse recent numbers might be less likely to report quickly, though CCJ does report increases as well as decreases — the report itself found homicide rose in 8 of the 30 cities with data, including Norfolk +64%, San Francisco +55%, Dallas +30%, Salt Lake City +20%, showing the sample is not filtered to only show declines). The city-level convenience sample is corroborated by two independent, non-overlapping data sources: (1) FBI's own national preliminary quarterly UCR data (all reporting agencies, not just 30-36 large cities), showing an even larger 23% national murder decline for the comparable period, and (2) Jeff Asher's Real Time Crime Index, an independently constructed sample of 566 agencies (~119M people, i.e., roughly a third of the U.S. population) showing murder down 18.7% through April 2026. Because these three sources use different city/agency compositions and methodologies yet converge on a decline in the high-teens-to-20s percent range, the risk that CCJ's 30-city sample happens to show a similar decline "by coincidence" is low — convergence across independently constructed samples is meaningful corroboration, not just coincidental similarity. That said, none of these sources is a true probability sample of the full U.S., so precision to the exact percentage point should be treated cautiously.

**Confidence:** Medium — the convenience-sample limitation is real and CCJ does not claim otherwise, but cross-validation against FBI's own national preliminary data and Asher's differently-constructed, larger-population RTCI sample substantially strengthens confidence that the direction and rough magnitude are real, not sample artifacts.

## Claim 5: Does Asher's Real Time Crime Index genuinely corroborate CCJ, or just coincidentally match?

**Verdict:** TRUE — genuine independent corroboration, not coincidence.

**Evidence:** Asher's RTCI (566 agencies, ~119M people through April 2026, murder down 18.7%) is built from a different, larger, and independently assembled set of reporting agencies than CCJ's 30-36-city sample, and is published separately (jasher.substack.com) from CCJ. The two efforts do not share a single dataset — CCJ aggregates large-city police department data on a recurring cadence; Asher's RTCI aggregates a broader mix of local agency NIBRS/local reporting. Both track directionally with the FBI's own preliminary quarterly national estimates (23% decline). The three independent measures (CCJ ~18%, RTCI 18.7%, FBI preliminary ~23%) cluster in the same range using different underlying agency sets and time windows, which is the signature of genuine convergent validity rather than shared-sample coincidence.

**Confidence:** High — the RTCI and CCJ samples are independently constructed and both align with FBI's own national preliminary data.

---

## Overall Assessment

The claim as stated is **well-supported and accurately sourced**. Both headline figures (18% year-over-year decline, 51% decline from the 2022 peak) are verbatim from CCJ's actual July 2026 mid-year report and are independently corroborated by FBI's own preliminary quarterly UCR data (which shows an even steeper 23% national decline) and by Jeff Asher's independently constructed Real Time Crime Index (18.7% decline across 566 agencies/119M people through April 2026). The "historic low" / "lowest since at least 1900" framing is correctly reported as conditional/hedged in the original claim, which matters: pre-1930s U.S. national homicide data (Death Registration Area coverage) is documented in the demographic/criminological literature as unreliable and likely biased, so "since 1900" precision is not fully defensible on the underlying data quality — but CCJ's own hedge ("will likely," "at least 1900") appropriately reflects that uncertainty rather than overstating it, and the qualitative claim that modern homicide rates are at generational/century-scale lows is well supported by modern (post-1930s) UCR-quality data. The 30-36-city sample is a convenience sample and not nationally representative in a statistical sense, but convergence with two independently constructed, differently-sourced datasets (FBI's own national preliminary numbers and Asher's larger RTCI) provides genuine — not coincidental — corroboration that the direction and rough magnitude of the decline are real national phenomena, not artifacts of CCJ's particular city selection.

**Overall confidence: Medium-High.** High confidence in the accuracy of the quoted statistics and their sourcing; medium confidence in the precise historical comparison ("since 1900") given known early-20th-century data quality limits, though the qualitative "historic low" characterization is well supported by modern data.
