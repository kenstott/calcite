# Fact-check: Better Markets, "FDIC Merger Rules Threaten Survival of Community Banks and the Main Street Economy"

Piece: press release dated 2026-11-23 by Phillip Basil (Better Markets), issued with its comment letter on the FDIC merger-review proposal. Note: the page was read through a summarizing fetch tool, so quotes are as extracted, not independently verbatim.

## Source caveat
Better Markets is an advocacy group. This is an opinion/advocacy release, not a news report. Most of it is argument, not checkable fact.

## Claims

| # | Claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The statement accompanies a comment letter on an FDIC proposal to substantially change merger review | Supported | FDIC proposed an overhaul of Bank Merger Act review on 2026-09-17 (Skadden, Davis Polk, ABA Banking Journal, American Banker). Comments were due 2026-11-23, matching the release date. |
| 2 | The proposal includes timelines that trigger automatic merger approvals ("deemed approval") and application acceptance | Largely supported | Law-firm summaries describe three tracks (rapid, expedited, standard) with clocks starting at receipt of a substantially complete application. Sheppard describes a de minimis category with deemed approval about five business days after requirements are met. Deemed approval is not clearly a feature of every track. I did not read the Federal Register text. |
| 3 | The FDIC wants to make review "a rubber-stamp process," especially for community banks | Opinion/characterization | The FDIC describes the proposal as modernizing and speeding review. Whether that amounts to a "rubber stamp" is a judgment. |
| 4 | Community banks' asset share fell from 45% (early 1990s) to 15% "last year" | Not checkable here (exact figures) | I could not retrieve the 1990s 45% figure. The direction is supported: FDIC's 2020 Community Banking Study puts community banks at about 12% of industry assets in 2019, and its 2012 study shows large banks' share rising sharply from 1984. The definition of "community bank" matters and the release does not give one. The 15% is plausible but the source is unnamed. |
| 5 | Large banks' share rose from under 20% to over 70% | Not checkable here (exact figures) | Directionally consistent with FDIC studies (banks over $10B rose from 27% in 1984 to 80% in 2011). The exact endpoints and size cutoff are not given. |
| 6 | Current rules favor the biggest banks, which lend least to Main Street | Opinion, unsourced | No data cited. |
| 7 | The FDIC "should rescind its proposal" | Opinion | Policy position. |
| 8 | Quotes attributed to Basil | Attributed correctly to the release | The release is the primary source for these. |

## Materially misleading or missing context
- The consolidation statistics are about decades of structural change. The release implies the FDIC proposal drives that trend, but the proposal is not shown to cause any of it. Much of the decline in community-bank share also reflects growth of large banks and a changing definition of "community bank."
- The release gives no day counts and no detail on the deemed-approval mechanism. Reported figures vary (Skadden: 90 or 150 days; American Banker: 90 or 180). Presenting it as simply "automatic approvals" omits that the clock starts only once an application is substantially complete, that the FDIC can ask for more information, and that Federal Reserve and DOJ review can still apply to larger deals.
- The headline says the rules "threaten the survival" of community banks. The text shows no evidence tying the proposal to community-bank failures. Some of the proposal's speed-ups could also help small banks that want to sell or merge.
- It does not mention that community banks and their trade groups may favor faster merger approvals.

## Rating
Two Pinocchios. The factual spine (an FDIC proposal exists, the comment deadline, the proposal includes timelines and some deemed approval) holds up, and the direction of the consolidation trend is right. The headline's "threaten survival" claim and the causal framing are unsupported, the precise statistics could not be sourced, and key counterpoints are left out.
