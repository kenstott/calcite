# Fact-check: CCJ July 2026 mid-year homicide report

## Bottom line
The core statistics are accurately characterized and corroborated by an independent source (Jeff Asher / Real Time Crime Index). The "lowest since at least 1900" framing is real but is the report's own explicitly hedged, conditional projection — not an established fact — and rests on historical data that is much thinner and less reliable pre-1930s than the phrase implies. The sample-size caveat is legitimate and the report itself flags it.

## 1. The 18% YoY decline — confirmed, and independently corroborated
- CCJ's mid-year report ("Homicide Likely Reaching a New Historic Low as Most Crime Rates Continue to Decline at Mid-Year," counciloncj.org, July 2026) states homicides in H1 2026 were down 18% from H1 2025, based on 30 of 36 sampled cities that provided homicide-specific data (215 fewer killings across those 30 cities).
- Independent corroboration: Jeff Asher's Real Time Crime Index (jasher.substack.com), using a much larger, differently-constructed sample (566 agencies, ~119 million people), found murder down 18.7% through April 2026 — essentially the same magnitude via a different data source and methodology. This is a strong independent cross-check and the two figures agree closely.
- Asher also notes 2025's decline "probably approached 18 or 19 percent," so a continued ~18% drop in 2026 is consistent with the ongoing multi-year trend, not an outlier data point.

## 2. The 51% decline from the 2022 peak — confirmed
- CCJ's report states homicides in H1 2026 were down 51% from H1 2022 (the recent peak, which followed the 2020-2021 pandemic-era homicide spike). This figure is in the same report as the 18% figure; I could not find a separate independent replication of the specific "51% from 2022 peak" statistic, but it is arithmetically consistent with the well-documented multi-year decline trend (2022 peak → successive ~18%/year-ish declines in 2023-2026) reported by Asher and other trackers (FBI preliminary data, CDC provisional mortality data have all shown a similar downward trajectory since 2022).

## 3. "Lowest since at least 1900" — real claim, but heavily hedged and historically thin
- This is CCJ's own framing, and it is a projection, not a finding: the report says 2025's rate (~4.0 per 100,000) would be "the lowest level recorded in law enforcement or public health data since at least 1900" IF confirmed by finalized FBI data — i.e., it is explicitly conditional/predictive, not asserted as settled fact. The report is careful about this; a lay reader could easily miss the hedge.
- The underlying historical data problem is real: reliable, nationally-representative U.S. homicide data does not really exist back to 1900 in the way the claim implies.
  - National mortality/vital-statistics reporting only began growing out of a small "death registration area" around 1900, covering roughly 10 states, DC, and a handful of cities — far from nationally representative, with major gaps (notably much of the South and West) for decades.
  - Uniform national crime reporting (FBI UCR) didn't start until 1930 (first IACP-published UCR) / 1933 (FBI-administered), and even then relied on voluntary agency submission with well-documented under-reporting and inconsistent participation for years afterward.
  - So a "since 1900" comparison is really stitching together fragmentary, non-representative early-20th-century vital statistics with FBI UCR data that only became reasonably robust from the 1930s-1960s onward. The comparison is directionally suggestive (current rates are very low by any historical standard) but should not be read as precise or as resting on a continuous, reliable century-long series.

## 4. Sample limitation — legitimate concern, and CCJ itself flags it
- The report covers 36 cities selected because they "consistently and rapidly published monthly data over the past nine years" — a data-availability/convenience sample, not a random or representative one. Only 30 of those 36 provided homicide data specifically.
- CCJ's own report states this limitation explicitly: "The study cities are not necessarily representative of every large American city or the nation as a whole," and that other sources may show different figures.
- That said, the pattern is not solely reliant on this one convenience sample — Asher's Real Time Crime Index (566 agencies, ~119M people, a far larger and more broadly constructed sample) shows a closely matching ~18-19% decline, which meaningfully strengthens confidence that the national trend is real even though the CCJ 36-city sample alone would be a weak basis for a national claim in isolation.

## Overall assessment
- The 18% and 51% figures are accurately reported and well-corroborated by independent data (Asher/Real Time Crime Index tracks closely).
- The "historic low," "lowest since at least 1900" language is the report's own explicitly conditional/hedged prediction pending final FBI data — accurately conveyed as a prediction if the reporting states it that way, but risks being overread as an established fact given how thin/non-representative pre-1930s U.S. homicide data actually is.
- The city sample is non-random and data-availability-selected, which CCJ acknowledges; on its own it would be a shaky base for national claims, but convergence with a much larger independent tracker (Real Time Crime Index) makes the overall directional claim (a real, large, multi-year homicide decline) credible, even if exact percentage points from the 36-city sample shouldn't be treated as precise national figures.
