Cleveland Scene's Ohio power-bill/governor's-race explainer checks out on the numbers; the daylight is in framing, not facts

Fact-check of every checkable factual claim and attributed quote, against EIA/AskAmerica data and primary documents (Ohio Auditor, PJM/Monitoring Analytics, OPSB, Ohio Supreme Court)

Summary

This Cleveland Scene/Ohio Capital Journal piece on Ohio electric bills and the 2026 governor's race holds up well. Every specific number I could directly verify against a fetched primary document or AskAmerica's own EIA data — the Ohio Auditor's price-increase figures, the PJM Independent Market Monitor's $29.4 billion data-center capacity-cost estimate, and the Ohio Power Siting Board's solar-denial count — matched the primary source exactly. Several other claims (FirstEnergy's rider collections, the PIPP eligibility threshold, SB 52's sponsor) were consistent with everything found via web search but weren't confirmed by a direct primary-document fetch in this session, so they're marked as such rather than certified. The one clean, directly-confirmed factual error found is small: the piece says Senate Bill 52 'became law in 2022,' when it actually took effect in October 2021 (the solar-denial data it cites does begin in October 2022, so the substance isn't wrong, just the year attached to the law itself). The more substantive concern is not factual but structural: the piece leans on a single expert source to both eviscerate one candidate's data-center plan ('doomed to fail,' 'not a serious plan') and largely endorse the other's ('a commonsense remedy'), which can read as a broader consensus than a single quoted source actually represents. Overall rating: 1 Pinocchio — essentially accurate reporting with one minor date slip and a real but non-fatal imbalance in how skeptically each candidate's plan is examined.

Independent evidence
8 denied solar projects, 1,293 MW total, denial dates Oct. 2022 - Jun 2026; SB 52 itself took effect Oct. 9, 2021, not 2022
  • Ohio Power Siting Board — Solar_Map_and_Stats.pdf
  • Ohio Capital Journal — Senate passes bill creating new hurdles for wind, solar development
Why
The OPSB's own published table lists exactly 8 denied solar cases totaling 1,293 MW (1.29 GW) — an exact match, read directly from the primary PDF, not estimated. But SB 52 was signed and took effect in October 2021, not 2022, per the Ohio Senate's own record and contemporaneous reporting — a genuine date error in the article. It happens that every one of the 8 recorded denials occurred from October 2022 onward, so the count and MW total the article is really conveying are correct even though the year it attaches to the law's enactment is off by about a year. Graded 'mostly true' for that reason: the headline data point is exact, the framing detail around it is not.

Method

I extracted every discrete, checkable factual assertion and attributed quote from the article (utility bill/rate statistics, PJM market mechanics, dollar figures for transmission and rider costs, legislative history, and the candidates' specific policy claims). I first searched AskAmerica's data catalog (search_catalog) for matching tables; where a table existed (energy.eia_electricity_prices for Ohio retail electricity rates, energy.pjm_capacity_auction_prices for PJM capacity clearing prices), I queried it directly. Where no AskAmerica table covers the underlying measure — PUCO's city-level bill dashboard, PJM's Independent Market Monitor capacity-cost attribution, and Ohio Power Siting Board case records — I fetched and read the primary source documents the article itself cites directly via web_fetch. For a few remaining claims where a direct fetch was blocked or not attempted, I relied on WebSearch corroboration and have labeled those claims accordingly rather than certifying them at the same confidence as the directly-fetched ones.

What AskAmerica's own data shows

AskAmerica's energy.eia_electricity_prices table (EIA API v2 retail sales data, Ohio, 2006-2024) shows Ohio's residential electricity price rising from 9.34 cents/kWh in 2006 to 15.99 cents/kWh in 2024 — a sustained, accelerating climb, especially 2021-2024 (12.77 to 15.99 cents/kWh, +25%). This independently corroborates the direction and general magnitude of the price pressure the article describes, even though the specific 116%/34% figures the article cites come from the state Auditor's own broader 'average energy price' series (2001-2026), which starts before AskAmerica's EIA coverage begins (2006) and runs to a partial 2026 that AskAmerica's annual table hasn't yet ingested. AskAmerica's pjm_capacity_auction_prices table separately confirms the scale of the capacity-price spike underlying the $29.4B data-center figure: the PJM RTO resource clearing price rose from $28.92/MW-day (2024/2025 delivery year) to $269.92 (2025/2026) to $329.17 (2026/2027) — a real, independently loaded data point consistent with the article's description of a sharply rising capacity market.

Claims verified against primary sources

See the claim table above for the six claims graded with sourcing and reasoning. In addition to those: the article's description of Ohio Senate Bill 52 as giving local officials, uniquely for wind and solar, power to block projects (with no equivalent authority over other generation types) is consistent with the bill's own legislative history and contemporaneous reporting found via web search. The AEP Ohio data center tariff, the OCC's 2023 FERC complaint, and the description of PJM's 'do no harm' supplemental-project review process are all consistent with the publicly available dockets and reporting the article links to, though I did not re-derive the $16 billion (2017-2025 Ohio transmission pass-through) or $10 billion (supplemental-process share) figures from a queryable dataset — no AskAmerica table currently carries PUCO's or PJM's project-level transmission cost-recovery data, so those numbers are reported here as the article's own sourced figures (attributed to Consumers' Counsel Willis and cleveland.com's reporting) rather than independently reproduced.

What's worth flagging beyond the facts

  • SB 52 enactment date: the article says the law 'became law in 2022'; it was actually signed and took effect in October 2021. The 8-project, 1.29 GW denial count itself is exactly right and all denials did occur from October 2022 onward, so the underlying data point survives, but the year attached to the law's enactment is simply wrong.
  • Asymmetric scrutiny: Ashley Brown, a single credentialed source (former PUCO commissioner), is quoted extensively and skeptically about Ramaswamy's free-power pledge ('doomed to fail,' 'It's not a serious plan') and used almost entirely affirmatively about Acton's data-center cost-recovery proposal ('a commonsense remedy'). Both sets of quotes appear to be accurately rendered, and the piece is transparent that Ramaswamy's campaign didn't respond to requests for detail — a legitimate reported fact. But presenting one expert's contrasting assessments of two different plans can read to a reader as broader expert consensus than it is. This doesn't make any individual fact false, but it does shape the piece's overall impression in a way worth naming.
  • Rounding language: 'in the past five years alone' for a CY2022-to-CY2026(YTD) span is a defensible but generous characterization of what is closer to four elapsed years.
