Rosen's EAD-rule warning checks out — and actually understates FWD.us's own figures

Fact-check of Sen. Jacky Rosen's April 29, 2026 Senate floor claims against the DHS/USCIS Oct. 30, 2025 Interim Final Rule, FWD.us's primary analysis, and AskAmerica/BLS employment data

Summary

Rosen's numbers hold up, and if anything are conservative. Her April 29, 2026 Senate floor remarks state, verbatim: 'More than three and a half million legally authorized workers nationwide could be impacted,' 'roughly six hundred thousand' in construction, and 'more than half a million' in hospitality. FWD.us's primary analysis (published April 9, 2026, using 2024 ACS data matched to FY2025 EAD-approval administrative data) puts the real figures at approximately 3.8 million total (3,765,000 in its published state-by-state table), 613,000 in construction, and 514,000 in leisure/hospitality — all consistent with, and in the case of the topline figure modestly higher than, what Rosen said. AskAmerica's own BLS employment data (econ.national_wages, 2025) shows these affected-worker counts are a small, plausible share of each industry's total workforce: about 7.1% of the 8.44 million U.S. construction workers and about 3.0% of the 17.25 million U.S. leisure/hospitality workers. The DHS/USCIS Interim Final Rule (Federal Register, Oct. 30, 2025) remains in effect: Rosen's Congressional Review Act resolution to overturn it failed on a party-line-leaning vote on April 29, 2026, and a pending federal lawsuit (Central District of California) has so far produced only a narrow protective order for the named plaintiffs, not a nationwide injunction.

What AskAmerica's warehouse could and couldn't answer directly

(a) Industry employment sanity check — available and checked. AskAmerica's econ.national_wages table (BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, national aggregates) gives 2025 U.S. employment of approximately 8,435,067 in construction (NAICS 23, all ownerships) and approximately 17,252,863 in combined arts/entertainment/recreation + accommodation/food services (NAICS 71+72, the leisure/hospitality supersector, all ownerships). Against these totals, the claimed ~600K construction and ~510-514K hospitality figures are entirely plausible — 7.1% and 3.0% of each industry's workforce, respectively, well within the documented range of foreign-born and pending-status worker concentration in these sectors, not an implausibly large share.

(b) Direct EAD/work-permit-holder counts by industry — not present in this corpus. A targeted search_catalog pass across immigration, work-authorization, EAD, asylum, TPS, and refugee terms returned no matching table or column in AskAmerica's warehouse; 'immigration,' 'asylum,' 'temporary,' and 'protected' all came back as explicitly unmatched search terms. This is a genuine coverage gap, not a data-quality issue — federal immigration-status/work-authorization microdata of this kind (USCIS administrative EAD-approval data merged with Census ACS) is not the kind of series this warehouse's public-data schemas carry. The claim was instead verified against FWD.us's own published primary analysis and the Federal Register rule text, fetched directly.

FWD.us's analysis, verified against the primary report

Fetched directly from fwd.us ('Ending Automatic Work Authorization Extensions Will Cost Billions,' published April 9, 2026). Verbatim: 'FWD.us estimates that ending the practice of automatically extending EADs is impacting as many as 3.8 million EAD workers in the U.S. workforce... including 1 in 20 in construction... and 1 in 25 in leisure and hospitality.' The bullet summary gives construction at 'about 610,000' and leisure/hospitality at 'about 510,000'; the report's detailed state-by-state table (which excludes states with fewer than 10,000 affected EAD holders, so its total is a slight undercount of the true national figure) sums to a TOTAL of 3,765,000, Construction 613,000, and Leisure and Hospitality 514,000 — both figures the user's claim cited exactly.

The category breakdown the user cited is also confirmed verbatim: 'refugees, those granted asylum and others applying for green cards (about 200,000 EAD workers); people who have applied for asylum (about 2.3 million); spouses of H-1B visa holders and other family members holding temporary or nonimmigrant visas (about 100,000); TPS holders and applicants (about 1.1 million), and thousands more.' Note: the user's claim describes the spouse category as '~100K H-4 spouses' — FWD.us's own text actually describes this group as spouses of H-1B holders and other nonimmigrant-visa family members (H-4 is the correct visa category for H-1B spouses, so this is a reasonable and standard shorthand, not an error).

Methodology, as stated by FWD.us: 'Augmented 2024 ACS data, projected to end of fiscal 2025,' with EAD holders identified by matching demographic/immigration-status assignments to 'actual EAD initial and renewal approvals from administrative data for each impacted group during fiscal year 2025.' This matches the methodology description in the user's claim exactly.

Did Rosen understate the figures?

Partially, and only for the topline number. Rosen's 'more than 3.5 million' is an open-ended lower bound that remains technically true of a 3.8 million actual figure — but it is a materially more conservative characterization, understating FWD.us's headline total by roughly 300,000 workers (about 8%). For construction and hospitality, however, Rosen's language ('roughly six hundred thousand' for 613,000; 'more than half a million' for 514,000) is an accurate, appropriately-hedged characterization of the FWD.us figures, not an understatement — 613,000 rounds naturally to 'roughly 600,000,' and 514,000 genuinely is 'more than half a million.' The user's framing that Rosen 'understated the figure' is accurate as a characterization of the topline number, but overstated as a characterization of the industry-specific figures, which she stated about as precisely as the underlying estimate supports.

Is the rule still in effect?

Yes, as of September 29, 2026. The DHS/USCIS Interim Final Rule was published in the Federal Register on October 30, 2025 (DHS Docket No. USCIS-2025-0271), ending automatic EAD extensions for renewal applicants filing on or after that date. Rosen's CRA resolution to overturn it failed in the Senate on April 29, 2026. A federal lawsuit (H-4 visa holders v. DHS, filed January 8, 2026, in the Central District of California) challenges the rule under the Administrative Procedure Act; a preliminary-injunction hearing was held June 24, 2026, and the court has so far issued only a limited protective order covering the named plaintiffs — not a nationwide injunction restoring automatic extensions. The rule remains in force nationwide outside that narrow carve-out.
