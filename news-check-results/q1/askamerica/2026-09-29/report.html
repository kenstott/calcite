Roll Call's $810M Pocket Rescission Story Checks Out on the Numbers; Its Supreme Court Claim Overstates a Preliminary Stay as a Final Ruling

Fact-check of Aris Folley, "White House cancels health, education funds approved by Congress," Roll Call, Sept 28, 2026

Summary

Roll Call's Sept. 28, 2026 story on the White House's $810 million "pocket rescission" is largely accurate on the facts it reports: the dollar total, the two named quotes, and the mechanics of a pocket rescission all check out against primary sources. Two issues merit a reader's attention. First, the article's five-item breakdown (summing to $746M) is incomplete, not wrong -- the White House's own fact sheet lists 11 line items totaling exactly $810M, and the $64M gap is fully explained by six smaller items (DHS grants, DOJ's Community Relations Service, a Treasury debt-relief program, the Minority Business Development Agency, and HHS's Office of Minority Health) the article never mentioned. Second, and more seriously, the article's closing line -- that in 2025 "the Supreme Court ultimately ruled that the administration had the right to cancel the money" -- materially overstates what the Court actually did. The Sept. 26, 2025 decision was a 6-3 stay of a lower-court preliminary injunction, issued on the emergency ("shadow") docket with no briefing or oral argument, finding only that the administration had made a sufficient showing that the Impoundment Control Act likely precluded the plaintiffs' APA claim. It was not a merits ruling that pocket rescissions are lawful, and the legal question remains open -- as underscored by the fact that GAO has separately opined pocket rescissions are unlawful, and that Sen. Collins is making the identical "unlawful" argument again in this very story. AskAmerica's own warehouse carries no table on OMB apportionments, impoundment actions, or Office of Refugee Resettlement appropriations, so every claim below was checked against independent primary sources rather than this connector's data.

Split rating note: because this piece quotes named officials (Sen. Collins, Rep. DeLauro) making their own legal assertions ("unlawful," "illegal"), this report grades two things separately: whether Roll Call accurately conveyed what happened and what was said (reporting fidelity), and whether the underlying assertions those officials and the administration made are themselves true (claims accuracy). These are not the same question, and collapsing them into one score would hide which one actually has the problem.

Claim-by-claim verdicts

🤥🤥○○ Fidelity: 2 of 4 Pinocchios

Roll Call quoted Collins and DeLauro verbatim and accurately, and its $810M topline figure is correct. But the piece's own narrative voice -- not attributed to any source -- states that the Supreme Court "ultimately ruled that the administration had the right to cancel the money" in the 2025 case. That is a significant mischaracterization of the Court's own action: a 6-3 emergency-docket stay of a preliminary injunction, decided without briefing or argument on a likelihood-of-success standard, not a merits ruling on the legality of pocket rescissions. Combined with an incomplete five-item spending breakdown that silently omits six line items (about $64M, 8% of the total) without flagging that the list isn't exhaustive, these are real reporting shortfalls in the piece's own fidelity -- significant omissions/exaggerations, count 2 -- even though nothing here rises to a fabricated figure or an invented quote.

🤥○○○ Claims Accuracy: 1 of 4 Pinocchios

The officials' own assertions -- Collins calling the cancellation an 'unlawful cancellation' and 'illegal actions,' DeLauro calling the freeze 'illegal' and the prior conduct 'unlawfully impounding funds' -- are stated as settled fact but are actually a contested, unresolved legal question. Their position has real backing (GAO has opined that pocket rescissions are not permitted under the Impoundment Control Act, and DeLauro has formally argued this in an amicus brief), so this is not a fabricated or baseless claim -- it lands at count 1, some shading/selective certainty about litigation that has not been finally decided on the merits, rather than a significant factual error.

Claim 1: The $810 million total and its breakdown (reporting fidelity)

Verdict: Accurate, but the article's breakdown is incomplete. The White House's Sept. 25, 2026 fact sheet ("President Trump Takes Historic Action to Eliminate Wasteful and Harmful Spending," whitehouse.gov) lists 11 rescission line items that sum to exactly $810 million: $567M (HHS non-citizen services incl. Office of Refugee Resettlement), $15M (DHS welfare-to-aliens grants), $10M (DHS aid-entry-of-aliens grants), $25M (Education migrant-student programs), $15M (DOJ Community Relations Service), $9M (Treasury Tropical Forest and Coral Reef Conservation Act debt relief), $10M (Minority Business Development Agency), $70M (international education exchange programs), $28M (HHS research), $56M (HUD housing counseling), and $5M (HHS Office of Minority Health). Roll Call's article reported only five of these ($567M + $25M + $70M + $28M + $56M = $746M), leaving $64M across six smaller items unexplained to readers. This is an editorial incompleteness in the piece's OWN reporting (fidelity), not a factual error in an attributed claim -- the headline $810M figure, and every dollar figure the article did cite, matches the primary source exactly.

Claim 2: Sen. Susan Collins's quote -- fidelity of the quote itself

Fidelity verdict: Accurate, verbatim. Collins's statement, posted to X, reads in relevant part: the OMB "intentionally withheld these funds for months to execute this unlawful cancellation of appropriations that were approved on a bipartisan basis and signed into law," and "I will work with my colleagues to address these illegal actions." This matches the article's quotation exactly -- Roll Call faithfully conveyed what Collins said and in what context. Collins also stated separately that "the independent Government Accountability Office has concluded that pocket rescissions are unlawful and not permitted by the Impoundment Control Act" -- context the article omits but which strengthens, rather than undermines, the piece's framing of bipartisan pushback.

Claims-accuracy verdict (is Collins's own assertion true?): Partially true / contested. Collins asserts the cancellation is "unlawful" and "illegal." This is a genuine, evidence-backed legal position -- GAO has issued opinions that pocket rescissions are not permitted under the Impoundment Control Act -- but it is not a settled legal fact. No court has issued a final merits ruling holding pocket rescissions illegal; the one Supreme Court action on point (2025) was a preliminary stay that, if anything, cut the other way procedurally (see Claim 4). Collins states her legal conclusion with more certainty than the law's current, unresolved status actually supports, though her position is far from baseless.

Claim 3: Rep. Rosa DeLauro's quote -- fidelity of the quote itself

Fidelity verdict: Accurate, verbatim. Confirmed directly against the House Appropriations Committee Democrats' Sept. 25, 2026 press release ("Trump Administration's 'Pocket Rescissions' are Illegal"), which quotes DeLauro: "Not only is freezing the money in this proposal illegal, but the administration has also clearly been unlawfully impounding funds all year just to get to this point." This is an exact match to the article's quotation -- fidelity is high.

Claims-accuracy verdict (is DeLauro's own assertion true?): Partially true / contested. Same underlying legal question as Collins's claim above: DeLauro asserts the freeze and prior impoundment were "illegal"/"unlawfully impounded," a position with real support (GAO's ICA opinion, DeLauro's own amicus brief in the 2025 case) but not an adjudicated fact -- no court has ruled on the merits that this conduct violated the ICA. Stated as flat fact rather than a contested legal position, though it is a defensible one.

Claim 4: The 2025 precedent and the Supreme Court's role -- the article's own reporting error (fidelity), not an attributed claim

Fidelity verdict: Materially misleading -- the piece's most significant problem. This claim is stated in Roll Call's own narrative voice, not attributed to any official: "the administration used the same maneuver last year to cancel about $4 billion in foreign aid without approval from Congress... [and] the Supreme Court ultimately ruled that the administration had the right to cancel the money." Because this is the reporter's own assertion rather than a quoted source's claim, it is graded entirely as a fidelity failure -- there is no separate "was the official right" question to ask here.

What actually happened: on Sept. 26, 2025, in Department of State v. AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition (and the consolidated Global Health Council v. Trump), the Supreme Court voted 6-3 along ideological lines to stay a district court's preliminary injunction that would have forced the administration to obligate roughly $4 billion (part of a broader $10.5 billion order) in foreign aid before the funds lapsed. This was decided on the Court's emergency ("shadow") docket -- Justice Kagan's dissent specifically objected that the majority acted "with scant briefing, no oral argument, and no opportunity to deliberate in conference." The majority's reasoning was that the administration had made a sufficient showing that the Impoundment Control Act likely precluded the plaintiffs from bringing this kind of claim under the APA -- a preliminary, likelihood-of-success finding for purposes of the stay, not a final adjudication that pocket rescissions are lawful. The underlying merits litigation was not resolved by this order.

Calling this "the Supreme Court ultimately ruled that the administration had the right to cancel the money" collapses an interim procedural stay into a final legal verdict. That distinction is not academic: GAO has issued its own opinion that pocket rescissions are not permitted under the Impoundment Control Act, and the same legality fight is happening again in the very story under review, with Collins and DeLauro both again calling the maneuver "illegal" and "unlawful." A reader taking the article's sentence at face value would reasonably conclude the legal question is settled in the administration's favor; it is not.

Claims not independently re-verified

The article's characterization of pocket-rescission mechanics (the 45-day clock, funds lapsing if the clock runs past fiscal year-end) and the White House's own "historic" framing of the action are accurately reported as characterizations/quotes from the relevant parties and were not graded as independent factual claims. The article's line that the House "is already out of town and not scheduled to return until after the November midterm elections" is a scheduling detail plausible on its face but was not independently re-verified against the House calendar; it does not bear on the article's core financial or legal claims.
