Related fact I did confirm: Harvard says a record 22.7 million renter households (49%) were cost-burdened in 2024.

## Overall rating: 1 Pinocchio (leaning "mostly accurate")

The verified anchor statistics (49% and 9.5 points, the BofA mortgage claims, and the Brooke history) are correct. I found no contradicted claim. The unconfirmed items are unconfirmed because of access limits, not because I found evidence against them.

## Potentially misleading framing

1. The piece frames the middle-income renter burden (49%) as a squeeze, but 49% is also the figure for all renters. The middle group is no worse off than the average renter. The sharpest burdens are at the bottom (reported 83%). The real change is the rise since 2019 (+9.5 points).
2. BofA's figures come from the bank's own customer data, not a representative national survey. They mix renters and mortgage holders and give no middle-income number. Presenting them next to Harvard's survey-based data suggests more confirmation of the "middle class" thesis than BofA provides.
3. The 30% threshold is a gross measure. Harvard itself says it may overstate burden for higher earners, which weakens the "even high-income renters" point. The article does disclose this.
4. Part of the "rents rose 38% vs incomes 28%" comparison depends on unverified numbers. Median figures also hide mix effects.
5. The McMillan anecdote closes with his "My Rent is Too Damn Low" quip. It is a light-hearted counterexample, and it gets more narrative weight than its relevance supports.
