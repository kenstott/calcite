FBI Crime Data Fact Check: Violent & Property Crime, 2024–2025

VERDICT: TRUE — confirmed against FBI primary source
Checked 2026-09-26 against the FBI Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) Program's own release, "UCR Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2025" (released August 2026).

Bottom line

Every figure in the claim matches the FBI's own primary release, word for word and number for number. The FBI's UCR Program reports that violent crime fell an estimated 9.3% from 2024 to 2025 — the largest year-to-year decline in the violent crime rate since the FBI began national estimations in 1936. Murder and nonnegligent manslaughter fell 18.1%, bringing the 2025 murder rate to 4.1 per 100,000 inhabitants, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest rate on record. Robbery fell 18.5%, aggravated assault fell 7.2%, and property crime fell 12.4%. All five figures are confirmed.

Methodology: which "FBI data" is this?

The 9.3%/18.1%/18.5%/7.2%/12.4% figures come from the traditional Crime in the United States (CIUS) national estimation methodology, not a raw NIBRS-only count. The FBI's own report explains that CIUS estimates blend aggregated Summary Reporting System (SRS) data — where only the single most serious offense in an incident is counted — with detailed NIBRS incident data converted into SRS-equivalent format, in order to represent the full U.S. population including non-reporting agencies. This is the FBI's long-standing headline estimation method (used since 1936) and is distinct from the newer "NIBRS-only" national estimates the FBI began publishing separately in 2021, and also distinct from BJS's forthcoming NIBRS-only "Crime Known to Law Enforcement, 2025" report and from the victimization-survey-based NCVS. The FBI explicitly cautions these methodologies are "analogous measures rather than one being more accurate than the other," and that its own numbers may not be directly comparable to statistics published elsewhere using different methodologies. There is no methodology mismatch in the claim as stated — the person appears to be citing the FBI's standard CIUS-based headline release, which is exactly what the FBI's press materials themselves used for these same percentages.

A subtlety worth flagging: the FBI's report separately states that, measured as rates per 100,000 inhabitants rather than raw offense volume, the violent crime rate fell 9.7% and the murder rate fell 18.5% year-over-year — both slightly larger than the 9.3%/18.1% volume-based figures because U.S. population also grew slightly in 2025. The claim's 9.3% and 18.1% numbers are the volume-based figures, which is also what the FBI itself leads with in its press release bullet points, so this is not an error, just a detail worth knowing if you see a "9.7%" or "18.5%" figure attached to violent crime/murder elsewhere.

Property crime detail (also confirmed)

Within the 12.4% property crime decline, the FBI's report breaks out: burglary decreased an estimated 15.8%; larceny-theft decreased an estimated 9.8%; motor vehicle theft decreased an estimated 22.7%. Burglary and larceny-theft rates each hit 20-year lows in 2025, and the robbery rate was the lowest in 20 years.

Caveats / what this does not mean

  • These are FBI Uniform Crime Reporting Program estimates based on crimes reported to police; they do not capture unreported crime (a gap the National Crime Victimization Survey is designed to address).
  • Agency participation is not 100%: 17,075 of ~19,558 enrolled agencies (87.3%) submitted data, covering 96.2% of the population; the rest is statistically estimated.
  • The "largest decline since 1936" claim is a rate-of-change superlative over FBI's own 90-year estimation series; it is not a claim that 2025 crime levels are the lowest ever (2025 violent crime and property crime rates are historically low but not literally all-time lows across every category — e.g., aggravated assault at 238.0/100,000 in 2025 is still above rates seen in 2013–2015).
