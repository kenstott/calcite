# Fact-check: Independent Institute "The U.S. Government's Worsening Deficit" (Craig Eyermann, Sept 23, 2026)

## Claim under review
Article (independent.org, 9/23/2026) states FY2026 deficit ~"$2.1 trillion" more spent than collected, revenue "over $5.2 trillion" by end of Sept 2026, a "$125.2 billion tariff refund ordered by the Supreme Court," a "$117 billion increase in interest payments... (14% increase)," and argues these two items ($242B combined) exceeded the total YoY increase in cumulative spending ($147B) through August — implying underlying spending growth (ex-interest, ex-refund) actually slowed.

## Verdict: Core dollar figures are accurate and traceable to Treasury/CBO. The "spending growth is slowing" conclusion is a selective, methodologically flawed framing — not supported once the periods and revenue/spending categories are matched correctly.

## Line-by-line verification

**$2.1 trillion deficit / $5.2 trillion revenue (FY2026 full-year)** — Accurate, consistent with CBO's own numbers.
- CBO (Aug 2026 update) projects the FY2026 deficit at ~$2.1T, revised up from earlier estimates largely because of the tariff-related revenue loss (Fortune, 8/10/2026: "Trump's $200 billion tariff hit swells budget deficit to $2.1 trillion for 2026, CBO confirms").
- Treasury MTS cumulative through August 2026 (11 months): receipts $4,845B, outlays $6,811B, deficit $1,966B. Adding a typical September (a strong revenue month, ~$400-450B, due to estimated-tax due dates) plausibly brings full-year receipts to "over $5.2 trillion" and the deficit to ~$2.1T. Both figures check out.

**$125.2 billion Supreme Court-ordered tariff refund** — Real event, right order of magnitude.
- Feb 20, 2026: Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorize the tariffs; refunds followed via CBP's CAPE process.
- Reporting as of late Sept 2026 (247wallst.com, 9/24/2026) put completed/certified refunds sent to Treasury at ~$122B, with ~$134.7B accepted for processing, and interest accruing on the refund pool (~$650M/month). $125.2B (~$122B + a few months' accrued interest) is a plausible, correctly-scaled figure for the article's Sept 23 writing date.
- CBO separately estimates FY2026 net customs revenue will run ~$250B (60%) below its pre-ruling February projection — a larger number because it includes both refunds and foregone future collections, not a contradiction of the $125.2B refund-specific figure.

**$117 billion / 14% interest-payment increase** — Accurate, but for a *10-month* window (through July 2026), not through August.
- CBO's July 2026 Monthly Budget Review states net interest on the public debt rose by $117B (14%) in the first 10 months of FY2026 vs. the same period a year earlier (net interest Oct 2025–July 2026 totaled $963B). This matches the article's figure exactly and is a real, CBO-confirmed number.
- Note: CBO's subsequent August (11-month) Monthly Budget Review shows net interest up $111B (12%) YoY over 11 months ($1,052B vs $941B) — the growth rate naturally moderates as more months are added; the article's $117B/14% figure is specifically the 10-month (July) figure, correctly stated as such.

## The "spending growth is slowing" framing — not supported; two problems

**1. Mismatched time windows.** The article compares a **10-month** interest figure ($117B through July) and a tariff-refund figure, against an **11-month** ("through August") total spending increase of $147B. These aren't the same period. Using CBO's own 10-month (July) data, which is the correct match for the $117B interest number, **total outlays were up $308B (5%) year-over-year** — more than double the $147B figure the article uses, and far larger than the combined $242B of interest-plus-refund. On a period-matched basis, there is no case that non-interest spending growth slowed; it was accelerating.

**2. The drop from $308B to $147B is a calendar artifact, not a spending slowdown.** Treasury/press analysis of the August 2026 MTS notes that Sept 1, 2025 fell on a weekend (pulling some FY2025 September payments into August 2025, inflating the FY2025 base) while Aug 1, 2026 fell on a weekend (pulling some FY2026 August payments into July 2026, deflating the FY2026 August total). This one-time timing shift mechanically narrows the YoY outlay comparison right around the July→August boundary — exactly where the article's $147B figure sits — without reflecting any actual change in federal spending behavior. Treasury's own MTS commentary states August FY26's deficit "would have been $9 billion larger" than August FY25 without the timing shift, underscoring how distortive the shift is.

**3. Category mismatch: the tariff refund is a revenue item, not a spending item.** Tariff refunds are booked in federal budget accounting as a reduction to customs receipts (revenue), not as an outlay (confirmed by refund/accounting reporting on the IEEPA refunds and by Treasury's June 2026 MTS treatment of the $49.2B in refunds that month as netted against gross customs receipts). The article adds a revenue-side item ($125.2B) to a spending-side item ($117B) and nets the $242B sum against a spending-only total ($147B). That is comparing unlike categories: only $117B of the $242B is actually "spending" in the first place, so the implied claim that all $242B represents spending pressure that "was absorbed while underlying spending still only grew $147B" overstates the case twice over — once by mixing periods, once by mixing revenue and outlay categories.

## Bottom line
- The headline numbers ($2.1T deficit, $5.2T revenue, $125.2B refund, $117B/14% interest) are each individually accurate and sourced to real Treasury/CBO data.
- The inference built on top of them — that these two items "exceeded" spending growth and imply underlying spending is decelerating — does not survive a period-matched, category-consistent comparison. CBO's own 10-month data (the correct match for the interest figure) shows outlays up $308B (5%) YoY, and part of the apparent August slowdown is a one-time calendar payment-timing shift rather than real fiscal restraint. This is selective framing: real facts, arranged to support a "spending discipline is improving" narrative that the underlying data does not actually establish.
