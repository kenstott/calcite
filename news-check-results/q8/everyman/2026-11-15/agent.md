# Fact-check: Fortune, "Hegseth's $1.5 trillion Pentagon budget ... global defense spending may still hit $8 trillion" (Sasha Rogelberg, 2026-11-15)

Method note: WebFetch returned only a summary of the article, not verbatim text, so quotes are checked as paraphrased by the fetch tool. Checks use web search and fetched pages only. Several findings rest on secondary reporting.

## Materially misleading elements

1. The headline and framing ("global defense spending hit/may hit $8 trillion") conflate a cumulative, decade-long, G7-only increment (about $8T) with an annual global total. Independent figures put annual global military spending at about $2.6-2.7T (2024-2025). Readers can easily come away believing annual global spending is $8T.
2. Pairing the $8T figure with the 3.8% of GDP projection makes it look like one calculation for a single year. The sources tie 3.8% to roughly 2030 and the $8T to a 10-year cumulative sum.
3. The $1.5T is a combined request (base plus reconciliation), not a single appropriations bill, which affects "will it pass" reasoning.
4. "Below $1 trillion" overstates Smith's stated position ("not above $1T"), and the contractor "investigation" is a planned oversight subcommittee, not an opened probe.
5. The $83B Ukraine aid figure is unsupported and inconsistent with the main public tracker.

## Rating: 2 Pinocchios (Washington Post scale)

The core political facts (the $1.5T request, Smith's objection, EU spending numbers) are accurate. The central headline number is badly framed, because it mixes cumulative G7 spending over a decade with annual global spending. A secondary number (the $83B in aid) cannot be verified. A fact-checker would call this "significant omissions and/or exaggerations" rather than outright falsehood.

## Not checkable here
Quotes from van Steenis, Bilmes, Patterson, and Stavridis's "structural" characterization; the Carlyle primary report; the Shangri-La claim (not searched); the exact article text.
