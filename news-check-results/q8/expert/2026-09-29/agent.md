# Fact-check: Independent Institute, "The U.S. Government's Worsening Deficit" (Craig Eyermann, Sept 23, 2026)

Source: https://www.independent.org/article/2026/09/23/governments-worsening-deficit/

## Claim 1: FY2026 deficit ~$2.1 trillion, revenue "over $5.2 trillion"

**Verdict:** Largely supported / plausible extrapolation.

**Evidence:** Treasury MTS data through August 31, 2026 (11 months of FY2026) shows: total receipts $4,845B, total outlays $6,811B, cumulative deficit $1,966B. Extrapolating September (historically the largest single receipts month, driven by corporate estimated-tax payments) to reach $5.2T total revenue and a ~$2.1T full-year deficit is directionally consistent with the trend, though it can't be verified to the dollar until the final September MTS is published (expected mid-October 2026). Notably, on a strict year-to-date (through-August) basis, the cumulative deficit was actually slightly *smaller* than the FY2025 comparison point ($1,966B vs. $1,973B), a fact the article does not mention — the "worsening" framing depends entirely on projecting the still-unreported month of September.

**Confidence:** Medium — the components (receipts, outlays, YTD deficit) are independently confirmed from Treasury data; only the September projection to $2.1T/$5.2T is not yet directly verifiable.

## Claim 2: "$125.2 billion tariff refund ordered by the Supreme Court"

**Verdict:** Correct order of magnitude, but the article mischaracterizes it as a spending item.

**Evidence:** The Supreme Court (Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, Feb. 20, 2026) held IEEPA does not authorize the tariffs; CBP/Treasury reporting shows roughly $122B in refunds certified and transmitted to Treasury as of Sept. 11, 2026, consistent with ~$125B by month's end. However, under standard federal budget/MTS accounting, customs duties are a **receipts** line item, and refunds of previously collected duties are booked as reductions to gross receipts (negative receipts), not as outlays. Treasury's own May/June 2026 MTS entries show this directly: June 2026 recorded $49.18B in refunds against only $23.63B collected, producing net *negative* customs revenue of ‑$25.56B for that month — booked entirely within the receipts side of the ledger. The article's own text states the refund and interest increase "increased the government's spending by $242 billion" — this is factually wrong for the refund component; the refund reduces revenue and widens the deficit, but it does not appear anywhere in the outlay/spending totals.

**Confidence:** High — this is a well-documented, unambiguous point of federal budget accounting, and the MTS's own line-item treatment of the refunds confirms it.

## Claim 3: "$117 billion increase in interest payments (14% increase)"

**Verdict:** Accurate, but for a different (shorter) time window than the spending figure it's compared against.

**Evidence:** This figure matches independent reporting (CRFB, Fortune) of gross interest-on-debt costs for the **first 10 months of FY2026** (October 2025–July 2026): ~$963B, up from ~$846B a year earlier, a $117B / ~14% increase. This is a real and well-supported figure — but it is explicitly a 10-month, not an 11-month ("through August"), figure.

**Confidence:** High.

## Claim 4 (central inferential claim): "$125.2B + $117B = $242B exceeds the $147B YoY increase in cumulative spending through August, implying underlying spending growth is slowing"

**Verdict:** Not supported — the comparison mixes mismatched time windows and mismatched budget categories (a revenue item vs. an outlay total), and the article's own wording explicitly (and incorrectly) recasts the tariff refund as a spending increase.

**(a) Time-window mismatch — CONFIRMED.** The $117B/14% interest figure is explicitly sourced to "the first 10 months of fiscal year 2026" (Oct 2025–Jul 2026). The $147B figure is the outlay increase "through August" (Oct 2025–Aug 2026), 11 months. Directly verified from Treasury MTS totals: FY2026 outlays through August = $6,811B; FY2025 outlays through August = $6,664B; difference = $147B (confirms the article's own number). But comparing a 10-month component (interest) against an 11-month total (spending) understates how much of that total increase interest alone would represent on a matched basis — using the same ~13–14% run rate for an 11th month, or independently reported 11-month interest figures (PGPF: net interest +8.9%/~$87B over 11 months; other trackers report gross interest cumulative surpassing $1T through August, up roughly $150B+ on some measures) — the "how much of $147B is interest" answer shifts materially depending on which 11-month interest figure and which interest concept (gross interest on debt vs. net interest, a BEA-style budget category that nets out certain intragovernmental flows) is used. The article picks the 10-month figure without noting the mismatch.

**(b) Category error — CONFIRMED, and this is the more serious problem.** The tariff refund is a receipts-side item, not an outlay. The $147B benchmark is an outlays-only figure (total spending). Adding a receipts-side item ($125.2B) to an outlay-side item ($117B) and then comparing that sum against an outlay-only total is not a valid budget-accounting operation — it implicitly treats "deficit-widening factors" as if they were all part of "spending," which they are not. The tariff refund's actual effect is to suppress *revenue* growth: receipts grew only $154B YoY through August ($4,845B vs. $4,691B); without the ~$125B in refunds already processed, receipts growth would have been roughly $270–280B, i.e., tariff collections net of refunds are a drag on the *revenue* side of the ledger, not evidence about restrained *spending*. The article's own sentence — "those two things increased the government's spending by $242 billion" — is the tell: it explicitly (and incorrectly) books a receipts item as spending.

**(c) Calendar/timing effect — real, but doesn't explain the YoY spending-growth figure.** August 1, 2026 fell on a Saturday, which (per Treasury/analyst commentary) shifted certain benefit payments from August into July 2026, making the August 2026 deficit look ~$9B smaller than it otherwise would have (commentary states the August FY26 deficit would have been $9B larger without the shift). This is a genuine, confirmed calendar effect — but because both July and August fall inside the same Oct–Aug 11-month cumulative window being compared, an intra-window shift between those two months doesn't change the 11-month cumulative total; it only reallocates dollars between adjacent months within the window. So this effect explains some of the dramatic narrowing of the *single-month* August YoY deficit comparison (the $178B narrower August deficit), but it is not a material explanation for the $147B cumulative 11-month spending-growth figure the article uses, and the article never raises or accounts for it either way.

**Confidence:** High (mismatch and category error) — both are directly verifiable from the article's own text and from Treasury MTS accounting conventions; the calendar effect is a real but secondary/non-explanatory factor for the specific $147B figure cited.

## Overall assessment

The "spending growth is slowing, masked only by two anomalies" framing is **not supported** once time windows and budget categories are matched correctly. Two distinct problems undermine the article's central inference:

1. It nets a **receipts-side** item (the $125.2B tariff refund) against a **spending-side** total ($147B in outlay growth), explicitly mislabeling the refund as having "increased spending." Under standard Treasury/MTS accounting, and as shown directly in Treasury's own monthly customs-duties receipts line, tariff refunds reduce revenue; they do not appear in outlays at all. This alone invalidates the $242B > $147B comparison as evidence about spending.
2. Even confined to the legitimate spending-side component (interest), the article compares a **10-month** interest increase ($117B, Oct–Jul) against an **11-month** total spending increase ($147B, Oct–Aug) — a mismatched-window comparison that the article does not disclose or reconcile.

The individual dollar figures the article cites (deficit ~$2.1T, revenue ~$5.2T projected, $125.2B tariff refund, $117B/14% interest increase, $147B spending increase) are each independently close to verifiable Treasury/CBO/CRFB data and are not themselves fabricated. The flaw is entirely in the inferential leap built on top of them: a genuine finding that interest costs are consuming a large share of nominal spending growth is real and worth reporting, but the claim that this — combined with a revenue-side tariff refund — proves "underlying spending growth is slowing" is an accounting and arithmetic error, not a supported conclusion.
