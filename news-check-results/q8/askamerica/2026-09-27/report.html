CNBC's Apple-Taction $5.7B verdict report checks out factually; split rating for a source-mislabeling issue

Every checkable fact and quote in CNBC's Sept. 26, 2026 article on Taction Technology v. Apple verified true against USPTO patent data and independent reporting

Summary

Split verdict: Fidelity 1/4, Claims Accuracy 0/4. Every discrete, checkable fact and quote in CNBC's Sept. 26, 2026 article on the Taction Technology v. Apple verdict is substantively true: the $5.7 billion damages figure, both patent numbers and their USPTO grant details, the case's 2021-2023-2025 procedural history, the trial timeline, the no-willfulness finding, and both quoted statements are all independently corroborated by named press sources (Bloomberg Law, AppleInsider) and, where a matching table exists, by USPTO's own patent_grants data -- hence Claims Accuracy scores 0. Fidelity scores 1, not 0, because CNBC mislabels Lance Yang as Taction's singular 'lead counsel': Bloomberg Law attributes a near-identical quote on this same verdict to a different Quinn Emanuel partner, Tigran Guledjian, and public records show multiple Quinn Emanuel partners worked the case with no one designated 'lead counsel' in any filing found. Separately, and outside the claim-by-claim scoring, the article's framing omits a materially relevant fact: Taction's suit was financed by outside litigation funders linked to Burford Capital.

What's materially missing

The article frames the case as a straightforward inventor-vs-Apple dispute. Bloomberg Law's reporting on the same verdict -- headlined around the litigation funding, not the damages number -- discloses that Taction's case was financed by Kenosha Investments LP and Gronostaj Investments LLC, with Kenosha identified in separate litigation as an indirect subsidiary of Burford Capital Ltd., a publicly traded litigation funder. Apple spent significant effort in discovery trying to expose this funding relationship (a judge even threatened funders with sanctions in July 2023 per Bloomberg Law's contemporaneous coverage). None of this makes any fact CNBC reported false, but omitting it leaves readers with an incomplete picture of who has a financial stake in the $5.7 billion outcome -- exactly the kind of context a professional fact-checker would flag as materially relevant even though the story's individual facts check out.
