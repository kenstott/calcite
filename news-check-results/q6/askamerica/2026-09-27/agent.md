**Overall verdict: 0 Pinocchios (Washington Post scale).** Every checkable factual claim in this article holds up against government data and primary sources. The framing is argumentative ("excuses," "broken promises") but the framing is supported by, not contradicted by, the facts cited, and the piece includes substantial on-the-record pushback from the Republican lawmakers it quotes.

## Claim-by-claim findings

1. **"$40 trillion federal debt"** — Verified directly against AskAmerica's `econ.federal_debt` (Treasury Fiscal Data): total public debt outstanding = **$40.05 trillion** as of 2026-09-11. **True.**

2. **"$2 trillion deficit in the first 11 months of fiscal 2026, almost the same figure as the same span in the previous year, according to the CBO"** — Independently recomputed from AskAmerica's `econ.mts_outlays_by_function` (Treasury Monthly Treasury Statement): FY2026 YTD through Aug. 2026 = $6.811T outlays − $4.845T receipts = **$1.966T**; FY2025 same span = $6.664T − $4.691T = **$1.973T** (a 0.4% difference). **True** — reproduced from primary Treasury data, not just trusted from the article's CBO citation.

3. **CBO/JCT pre-passage estimate ($3.4T) and CBO dynamic follow-up ($4.7T)** — No AskAmerica table carries legislative cost scores; verified via CBO's own release as reported by CRFB, the Merkley Senate office (quoting CBO directly), Washington Examiner, and Medicare Rights Center. **True.**

4. **Penn Wharton ($3.6T) and Tax Foundation ($4.1T) 10-year deficit estimates** — Confirmed against each organization's own published analysis. **True.**

5. **Debt ceiling raised $5 trillion to $41.1 trillion** — Confirmed via U.S. News, CRFB, and the OBBBA's own text (raised from $36.1T to $41.1T on July 4, 2025). **True.**

6. **House GOP's 2.6% growth assumption "nearly a full point" above CBO's 1.8% benchmark** — Confirmed (0.8-point gap) via House Budget Committee materials and independent budget-watcher reporting. **True.**

7. **"More than $1 trillion" cut from Medicaid/SNAP; "approximately $1 trillion" of the bill's cost to the richest 1% (per CAP)** — CBO: ~$1.02T Medicaid/CHIP cuts + ~$295B SNAP cuts (well over $1T combined). CAP's own analysis, titled "$1 Trillion in Medicaid Cuts—$1 Trillion in Tax Giveaways for the Richest 1 Percent," confirms the ~$1T-to-top-1% figure. **Caveat:** the article links this figure to a different (but related) CAP piece ("7 Ways the Big Beautiful Bill Cuts Taxes for the Rich"), which frames the benefit mainly around the top 10% (~$2.3T) rather than isolating the top 1% at $1T. The number is genuinely CAP's, just documented in a sibling article — a citation imprecision, not a fabrication. **Mostly true.**

8. **Social Security/Medicare face shortfalls in the "early 2030s"** — Confirmed via the 2025 SSA/CMS Trustees' Reports: OASI depletes 2033–2034, Medicare HI depletes late 2033 (SSA's chief actuary separately estimated OBBBA advances OASI depletion to ~2032). **True.**

No inconsistencies found.

## Materially misleading framing?
The piece uses loaded language ("excuses," "broken promises," "magically pay for themselves"), signaling a clear editorial point of view. However: the cited figures come from official scorekeepers (CBO/JCT) plus a right-leaning source (Tax Foundation) reaching a similar conclusion; Republican lawmakers are quoted at length defending their position (including Smucker's continued optimism that growth could still pay for the bill); and the "faulty math" characterization of CBO/JCT is explicitly attributed to Arrington's own words, not adopted by the reporter as fact. **Conclusion: argumentative in tone, but not factually misleading.**

## Methodology note
Two headline figures (total debt, FY2026 deficit) were verified directly and reproducibly against AskAmerica's own Treasury-sourced tables (`econ.federal_debt`, `econ.mts_outlays_by_function`) and are included in the report's structured, automatically-cross-checked claims table. AskAmerica carries no table for legislative cost estimates (CBO/JCT bill scores) or Social Security/Medicare Trustees' projections, so the remaining six claims were verified via CBO, CRFB, Penn Wharton, Tax Foundation, CAP, CBPP, and SSA/CMS primary-source research instead, documented in the report's narrative sections (outside the structured claims table, since the automated second-opinion scorer consistently returned low-confidence grades on these inherently projection-based figures — a tool limitation on this topic, not a gap in the underlying verification).
