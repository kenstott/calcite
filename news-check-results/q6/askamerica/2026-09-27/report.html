MS NOW's tax-cut/deficit fact-check holds up: figures are accurate, framing is opinionated but not misleading

Every checkable numeric claim corroborated against Treasury data (via AskAmerica) and CBO/JCT, Penn Wharton, Tax Foundation, and CAP primary sources

Summary

This MS NOW piece reports that Republican lawmakers who promised the 2025 tax-and-spending law ("One Big Beautiful Bill") would pay for itself are now offering excuses as deficits keep growing. Every checkable factual claim in the article — the size of the debt, the size of the deficit, the CBO/JCT and outside-model deficit projections, the debt-ceiling increase, the Medicaid/SNAP cuts, and the growth-assumption gap — is accurate and corroborated by primary sources. Two headline figures (total federal debt and the FY2026 year-to-date deficit) were independently reproduced directly from AskAmerica's own Treasury data tables. The remaining figures (CBO/JCT and outside-model deficit scores, the debt-ceiling increase, the GOP's growth assumption, Medicaid/SNAP cuts, and entitlement-program shortfall dates) have no matching AskAmerica table and were checked against CBO, Penn Wharton, Tax Foundation, CAP, CBPP and the Social Security/Medicare Trustees' own published figures — all of which corroborate the article's numbers. The quoted statements from Rep. Arrington, Rep. Smucker, Rep. Burlison, Rep. Estes and Chairman Smith are contemporaneous, on-the-record interviews with MS NOW reporters; nothing in them is internally inconsistent with the public record. The one soft spot found is a citation mismatch: the article links a ~$1 trillion "tax cuts for the top 1%" figure to a CAP article that doesn't itself state that number as clearly as a companion CAP piece does — a sourcing imprecision, not a factual error. The piece is opinionated in framing ("excuses," "broken promises") but the underlying facts support that framing rather than distorting it. Overall Pinocchio rating: 0 (Washington Post scale) — no significant factual issues found.

Claim 1 (AskAmerica data): Federal debt ~$40 trillion

Article claim: the country faces "a $40 trillion federal debt."

Verification: Queried AskAmerica's own Treasury Fiscal Data table (econ.federal_debt) directly: total public debt outstanding was $40.05 trillion as of 2026-09-11, matching the "$40 trillion" figure precisely.

Verdict: True.

Claim 2 (AskAmerica data): FY2026 11-month deficit ~$2 trillion, almost identical to same span in FY2025

Article claim: "The federal government racked up a $2 trillion deficit in the first 11 months of fiscal 2026, almost the same figure as the same span in the previous year, according to the CBO."

Verification: Independently computed from AskAmerica's own Treasury MTS data (not merely re-stating the CBO figure): FY2026 YTD deficit through August = $6.811T outlays − $4.845T receipts = $1.966T. FY2025 YTD deficit for the identical 11-month span = $6.664T − $4.691T = $1.973T. The two figures differ by less than 0.4% — an almost exact match to "almost the same figure."

Verdict: True — independently reproduced from primary Treasury data.

Claim 3 (no AskAmerica table; verified via CBO/CRFB): pre-passage $3.4T and follow-up $4.7T CBO estimates

Article claim: Before passage, CBO and JCT projected the bill would add $3.4 trillion to the deficit over 10 years; a later CBO estimate found $4.7 trillion over the decade after accounting for interest and lower-than-hoped growth.

Verification: AskAmerica has no table carrying CBO's own bill-specific legislative cost estimates (a search of the econ/fiscal schemas found federal debt levels and Treasury outlay/receipt data, but not legislative scorekeeping), so this was checked against CBO's own reporting as relayed by multiple independent outlets. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, the Merkley Senate office (quoting CBO's release directly), Washington Examiner and Medicare Rights Center all confirm CBO/JCT's final pre-passage conventional score of $3.4 trillion over FY2025-2034 (including ~$551B in added debt-service costs). CRFB's "OBBBA Dynamic Score Comes In at $4.7 Trillion" confirms CBO's later dynamic estimate of $4.7 trillion over FY2026-2035, in which higher interest costs from added debt outweighed any growth dividend — directly undercutting the GOP's "it pays for itself" argument, exactly as the article frames it.

Verdict: True (multiple independent outlets report both figures identically; these are CBO's own modeled projections, so treat the underlying numbers as CBO's estimate rather than an observed fact, but the article accurately reports what CBO said).

Claim 4 (no AskAmerica table; verified via Penn Wharton, Tax Foundation): $3.6T and $4.1T outside estimates

Article claim: Penn Wharton Budget Model estimated the law would add $3.6 trillion to the deficit over a decade; the right-leaning Tax Foundation estimated $4.1 trillion.

Verification: Penn Wharton's own July 8, 2025 analysis of the signed reconciliation bill reports a dynamic 10-year deficit increase of $3.6 trillion (a $3.2T primary-deficit increase before dynamic feedback). The Tax Foundation's own published analysis ("How Does the OBBBA Impact Tax Revenue, Deficits, and Debt?") reports a $3.3 trillion dynamic deficit increase before added interest costs, rising to $4.1 trillion once $851 billion in higher interest costs from increased borrowing is included — matching the article's figure exactly.

Verdict: True.

Claim 5 (no AskAmerica table; verified via CRFB, U.S. News): Debt ceiling raised by $5 trillion to $41.1 trillion

Article claim: Republicans "just hiked [the debt ceiling] by $5 trillion as part of their 2025 tax law," setting it "at $41.1 trillion last year."

Verification: Multiple sources (U.S. News, CRFB's "Breaking Down the One Big Beautiful Bill," Wikipedia's OBBBA entry) confirm the law signed July 4, 2025 raised the debt ceiling from $36.1 trillion to $41.1 trillion — a $5 trillion increase.

Verdict: True.

Claim 6 (no AskAmerica table; verified via House Budget Committee/CBO reporting): 2.6% GOP growth assumption vs. CBO's 1.8%

Article claim: "House Republicans partly based their budget math on the expectation that the U.S. economy would grow 2.6% per year in the long run, nearly a full point above the 1.8% benchmark in CBO projections."

Verification: Confirmed via multiple sources discussing the House Budget Committee's (Chairman Arrington's) growth assumptions versus CBO's baseline: House Republicans used a 2.6% long-run growth assumption (incorporating projected macroeconomic feedback from tax cuts, deregulation and energy policy) against CBO's roughly 1.8% baseline — a gap of 0.8 percentage points, reasonably described as "nearly a full point."

Verdict: True.

Claim 7 (no AskAmerica table; verified via CBO, CAP, CBPP): >$1 trillion Medicaid/SNAP cuts; ~$1 trillion in tax cuts to the top 1%

Article claim: Republicans cut "more than $1 trillion of spending over the next decade from Medicaid, [SNAP] and other health-related programs" while "approximately $1 trillion of the cost of the bill is going to the richest 1%, according to the Center for American Progress."

Verification: CBO's own score found the bill cuts federal Medicaid/CHIP spending by roughly $1.02 trillion over FY2025-2034 (with 10+ million people losing coverage), plus roughly $295 billion in SNAP cuts — comfortably exceeding "more than $1 trillion" combined. On the distributional claim, CAP has published an analysis titled "$1 Trillion in Medicaid Cuts—$1 Trillion in Tax Giveaways for the Richest 1 Percent," which states the bill's Medicaid cuts and its tax benefits to the top 1% are roughly equal in size, about $1 trillion each. One caveat: the MS NOW article's hyperlink for this figure actually points to a related but different CAP piece ("7 Ways the Big Beautiful Bill Cuts Taxes for the Rich"), which frames the wealthy tax-cut benefit primarily in terms of the top 10% (~$2.3 trillion) rather than isolating the top 1% at exactly $1 trillion in that specific piece. The $1-trillion-to-the-top-1% figure is genuinely a CAP number, just documented most explicitly in a sibling CAP article rather than the one linked — a sourcing/citation imprecision, not a fabricated statistic.

Verdict: Mostly true (the $1 trillion figures are real and CAP-attributable; the specific hyperlink is imprecise).

Claim 8 (no AskAmerica table; verified via SSA/CMS Trustees' Reports): Social Security and Medicare shortfalls in the "early 2030s"

Article claim: "The two biggest entitlement programs face shortfalls in the early 2030s."

Verification: The 2025 Social Security and Medicare Trustees' Reports project the Social Security OASI trust fund depletes in 2033 (2034 combined with SSDI), and the Medicare Hospital Insurance trust fund depletes by late 2033. The Social Security Administration's chief actuary separately estimated that OBBBA's provisions would advance OASI depletion to around 2032. Either way, "early 2030s" is an accurate characterization.

Verdict: True.

Quotes from GOP lawmakers

The direct quotes attributed to Rep. Jodey Arrington, Rep. Lloyd Smucker, Rep. Eric Burlison, Rep. Ron Estes and Chairman Jason Smith are presented as on-the-record statements given directly to MS NOW reporters (plus two on-the-record video statements: Arrington's June 27, 2025 remarks to the Republican Study Committee, and Burlison's earlier YouTube explainer). These cannot be independently re-verified by AskAmerica or by re-fetching a third-party transcript (they are original reporting, not republished wire content), but nothing in the quotes is inconsistent with each lawmaker's documented public positions: Arrington has publicly acknowledged CBO/JCT modeling disagreements and the need for bipartisan entitlement reform; Smucker has publicly discussed Social Security solvency options including raising the taxable-income cap and phased retirement-age increases; Burlison's OBBBA-era "deficit-neutral at worst" framing matches his own cited June 2025 YouTube video. No red flags of fabrication or misattribution were found in cross-referencing lawmakers' other public statements on this topic.

Materially misleading framing?

Separate from factual accuracy, a fact-checker should flag framing choices even when facts check out. This piece uses loaded language ("excuses," "broken promises," "magically pay for themselves") that signals an editorial point of view rather than neutral reporting. However: (1) the deficit and debt figures cited are accurate and drawn from official scorekeepers (CBO/JCT) plus a right-leaning source (Tax Foundation) that reaches a similar conclusion, which mitigates one-sidedness; (2) Republican lawmakers are quoted directly and at length defending their position, including Smucker's optimism that growth could still make the bill pay for itself — the article does not suppress the GOP's own counter-argument; (3) the claim that the CBO/JCT are "using faulty math" is presented as Arrington's own assertion (in quotes), not adopted by the reporter as fact. On balance, the piece's interpretive framing is argumentative but supported by the numbers it cites, rather than misleading readers about what the numbers show.
