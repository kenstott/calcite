Summary

This MS NOW piece checks out in full. Every dollar figure attributed to a named third party — CBO/JCT's pre-enactment $3.4 trillion conventional score, CBO's follow-up $4.7 trillion dynamic estimate, Penn Wharton's $3.6 trillion, and the Tax Foundation's $4.1 trillion — was verified directly against each organization's own primary-source publication (fetched and read in full, not taken from a secondary summary). All four are genuine whole-bill deficit-added estimates, correctly attributed. The $40 trillion federal debt figure and the ~$2 trillion FY2026 deficit pace are both confirmed directly against AskAmerica's own econ.federal_debt table and CBO's Monthly Budget Review. The 2.6%-vs-1.8% GDP growth-assumption gap and the $5 trillion/$41.1 trillion debt-ceiling increase are also confirmed accurate.

Correction note: an earlier pass at this fact-check, relying on a secondary CRFB summary that described Tax Foundation's estimate as covering only "OBBBA's major tax provisions," concluded the article's $4.1 trillion figure conflated a narrower tax-only revenue-loss estimate with a whole-bill deficit figure. Fetching Tax Foundation's own primary-source page directly showed this was wrong: Tax Foundation's published $4.1 trillion IS already its own whole-bill dynamic deficit-increase estimate (tax revenue effects combined with CBO's estimated spending cuts, plus $851 billion in dynamic interest costs), matching the article's claim precisely. This is corrected here.

Materially misleading even where facts check out?

The article's side-by-side presentation of four deficit-added numbers ($3.4T, $4.7T, $3.6T, $4.1T) is not misleading in substance — all four are genuine whole-bill deficit estimates — but the underlying windows differ slightly (CBO's $3.4T conventional score covers FY2025-2034; its $4.7T dynamic follow-up covers FY2026-2035; Penn Wharton and Tax Foundation both use the FY2025-2034 window). None of this is disclosed to the reader, and a careful comparison of the four bars would benefit from knowing they aren't all measured over the identical 10-year span. This is a minor methodological omission, not a factual error, and does not change the article's well-supported core thesis: multiple credible forecasters across the ideological spectrum — including the right-leaning Tax Foundation — agree the 2025 reconciliation law adds trillions of dollars to the deficit, contradicting GOP promises that it would pay for itself.
