# Fact-check: NAHB Sept 25, 2026 post on immigration enforcement and homebuilding

**Bottom line: The figures are essentially accurate as quoted from NAHB's own post, but nearly every number in the claim traces back to NAHB or its affiliate HBI's own advocacy research rather than an independent government or academic body. The underlying government data point (job openings) roughly supports the 300,000 figure. The "1.2 million" housing shortage is NAHB's own (low-end, methodology-dependent) estimate, not a consensus government figure. The HBI study is real but industry-commissioned, and its topline dollar figure is understated by NAHB's rounding.**

## 1. "Housing shortage of about 1.2 million units"

- This is NAHB's own figure, from its "Eye on Housing" analysis (nahb.org/blog/2026/02/the-housing-shortage-explained-by-2024-data), based on 2024 vacancy-rate data. NAHB calculates the shortage as the number of vacant units needed to return current rental/homeowner vacancy rates to their long-run historical average — a narrow, methodology-specific approach.
- This is the **low end** of a wide range of published shortage estimates. Other analyses (Up for Growth, Zillow, Realtor.com, the National Association of Realtors, and even the Trump White House) put the national housing shortfall anywhere from ~1.2 million to 10+ million units, with a rough cross-source consensus around 4–5 million, depending on whether pent-up household formation and obsolete/aging stock are counted.
- **Verdict: Accurately quoted, but it is NAHB's own conservative estimate, not an independent or consensus government figure, and the range across sources is enormous. NAHB citing the low end of its own estimate range understates the more commonly cited housing deficit.**

## 2. "Construction workforce shortage of 300,000 workers"

- NAHB attributes this to "government data" without naming the specific report in the post itself.
- The closest matching independent, government series is the BLS JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) measure of construction job openings (FRED series JTS2300JOL), which stood at **326,000 in July 2026** (most recent available, updated Sept 1, 2026) — in the same range as "about 300,000." Note job openings ≠ a "shortage" in a strict economic sense (openings reflect employer demand, not proof no qualified worker exists), but it's a defensible, roughly-matching independent data point.
- Separately, the trade group Associated Builders and Contractors (ABC) — an industry association, not government — estimated the industry needs to attract **349,000 net new workers in 2026** to meet demand (down from higher prior-year estimates), a different and somewhat larger figure using a different methodology.
- **Verdict: In the right ballpark and traceable to a genuine BLS data series, though NAHB doesn't cite the source directly and the term "shortage" conflates job openings with an unfilled-workforce gap.**

## 3. Immigrant workers are "more than one-quarter" of the construction workforce

- Well-supported by NAHB's own analysis (citing Census/ACS-derived data): immigrant workers were **26.3% of the total construction workforce in 2024**, a record high, and about **one-third of construction trades workers** specifically (e.g., 57% of drywall/ceiling tile installers, 56% of plasterers/stucco masons, 53% of roofers and painters, 43% of construction laborers).
- This NAHB estimate is consistent with independent reporting citing Census Bureau/American Community Survey data (e.g., Scotsman Guide, immigration-policy outlets), so it is not solely an NAHB-manufactured figure — the underlying Census data is public and independently reproducible.
- **Verdict: Accurate and independently corroborable via Census/ACS data, even though NAHB is the one packaging and publicizing it.**

## 4. Home Builders Institute (HBI) study, June 2025

- The study is real: commissioned by HBI (NAHB's workforce-development affiliate) and **conducted by the University of Denver**, with NAHB supplementing the analysis. This is industry-commissioned research, not an independent academic or government study, though it used an outside university team.
- Reported figures:
  - **Construction delay**: study reports an unweighted average increase of **1.98 months** (NAHB's post rounds this to "two months" — accurate rounding, and smaller builders (<100 homes/year) see a larger delay, ~2.36 months).
  - **19,000 fewer homes built annually**: matches the study — approximately 19,000 single-family homes were not built in 2024 due to the labor shortage.
  - **$10 billion annual cost**: the study's actual topline figure is **$10.806 billion/year** ($2.663B in higher carrying costs + $8.143B in losses from unbuilt homes). NAHB's "$10 billion" is a reasonable round-down, not a misstatement, though the more precise total is closer to $10.8 billion.
  - **Scope caveat**: the study is limited to the **single-family home building sector** — it does not cover multifamily construction, despite being cited as a general "housing industry" cost.

## Overall assessment

- The individual figures NAHB cites are **not fabricated** — the 1.2M shortage, 300K worker gap, >25% immigrant share, and HBI's 2-month/19,000-home/$10B estimates are all traceable to real reports and are quoted with reasonable (if generous) rounding.
- However, **three of the four claims originate wholly from NAHB or its own affiliate HBI** (the 1.2M shortage estimate, and the HBI/University of Denver study's headline numbers), making this substantially an industry trade group citing its own commissioned research and estimates to support its own policy advocacy on immigration enforcement. Only the immigrant workforce-share figure (Census/ACS-derived) and the construction-job-openings figure (BLS JOLTS) have clear independent government/statistical backing, even though NAHB is again the one packaging them.
- The "1.2 million" shortage figure in particular should be flagged: it's the **low end** of a wide range of published estimates (1.2M–10M+), so citing it without noting that range, while making an argument that housing scarcity is severe, is somewhat in tension with NAHB's broader framing (a bigger shortage estimate would actually strengthen NAHB's argument, so this isn't cherry-picking in NAHB's favor — but it does show the figure is one contested estimate among many, not a settled number).
- The causal claim in the framing sentence — that enforcement is "delaying home construction and raising costs" — is a reasonable characterization of anecdotal and study evidence, but it should be read as an industry group's characterization of a policy effect, not a peer-reviewed causal finding; the HBI study measured labor-shortage effects generally (of which immigration enforcement is one contributing driver), not a study designed specifically to isolate 2026 immigration-enforcement effects.
