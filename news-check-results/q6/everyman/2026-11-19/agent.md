# Fact-check: Grist, "A monstrous El Niño has arrived. Here's what it means for where you live" (Nov 9, 2026)

Limits: I used WebFetch, which returned a summary of the article rather than its full text. Quotes were not verified verbatim. Where I could not reach a primary source, I relied on secondary reports.

## Overall rating: 1 Pinocchio (mostly accurate, with a few overstated or unverified points)

## Claims checked

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| El Niño is "already the strongest on record" | **Overstated** | The NOAA CPC discussion of Nov 12, 2026 (after publication) does not say this. It gives about a 60% chance of a historic event exceeding all El Niños since 1950. Niño-3.4 is +2.5C. The Aug-Oct ONI was +2.46C, third highest. Not yet confirmed as the record. |
| It may not peak for another month or two | Supported | CPC expects strong conditions through winter, with weakening possible in early 2027. |
| Strong to very strong through winter, NOAA outlook Dec-Feb | Supported | CPC: above 79% chance of strong-to-very-strong through Jan-Mar 2027. |
| About 450,000 heat deaths | Supported with caveats | The Climate Impact Lab at the University of Chicago projects about 451,000 additional heat deaths, June 2026 to Feb 2027. It is a preliminary, non-peer-reviewed modeling projection, not a count. Grist's "scientists estimate ... could kill" wording is fair. |
| Newsom declared a state of emergency | Supported | Statewide proclamation on Sep 21, 2026 (gov.ca.gov, KPBS, KQED). |
| Warmest September on record in the Midwest, 132-year NOAA record | **Partly supported** | NOAA reported the warmest September on record for the contiguous US. Eight states set records, including Illinois, Missouri, Kansas, Arkansas and Texas. Local stations show variation: Indianapolis was a record, Chicago tied 10th. I could not find the "132-year" regional figure. |
| Ocean holds about 1,000 times more heat than the atmosphere (Art Miller, Scripps) | Plausible | This is a standard approximation, but I did not verify that Miller said it. |
| El Niño recurs every 3 to 5 years and lasts 9 to 12 months | Consistent with NOAA's usual description (2 to 7 years is the more usual NOAA range). | |
| Drought in the Amazon and extreme rain in the Greater Horn of Africa | Consistent with the typical El Niño teleconnections, but not verified for 2026. | |
| Regional outlooks (Alaska, Hawaii, Midwest, Northeast, Plains, Northwest, Southwest, Southeast, California) | Consistent with standard El Niño winter patterns | The article hedges appropriately: no one can reliably predict local weather a month ahead. |
| Attributed quotes (Stuefer, Longman, Kluver, Callahan, Pugh, Ganguly, Hunt, Edwards, Ritchison, Bocinsky, Genuise, Meyer, Goble, Watson, Hoell, Nielsen-Gammon, Springhorn; Texas Railroad Commission) | **Not checkable here** | These came from interviews and I could not retrieve the full text. The speakers' roles are plausible. |

## Potentially misleading
- "Strongest on record" is stated as fact when NOAA frames it as a probability (about 60%) of becoming historic.
- The 450,000 figure is a preliminary projection of heat deaths, and it may be read as a settled number.
- The Midwest record is uneven across the region (strong in the south and center, middling in the north).
- The headline implies certainty of local impacts, but the article does say that regional forecasts are probabilistic.
