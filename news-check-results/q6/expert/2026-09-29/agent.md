# Fact-check: NAHB Sept 25, 2026 post on immigration enforcement and construction labor

Source checked: NAHB blog, "NAHB Responds as Immigration Enforcement Keeps Legal Workers Off Job
Sites" (nahb.org/blog/2026/09/immigration-enforcement), plus corroborating coverage (RISMedia,
HousingWire, BCASWI) and primary underlying sources (HBI/Univ. of Denver study PDF, NAHB's own
housing-shortage methodology post, BLS JOLTS releases, other housing-shortage estimates).

---

## Claim 1: "Stepped up immigration enforcement has discouraged legally authorized workers from
reporting to jobsites, delaying home construction and raising costs"

**Verdict:** Unverifiable as stated / Anecdotal, not independently substantiated.

**Evidence:** This is NAHB's characterization of member reports, not a measured, data-backed
finding. No independent survey, BLS series, or third-party study was found that quantifies
enforcement-driven absenteeism on jobsites. Related reporting (HousingWire "ICE raids strain
homebuilder labor, cycle times, and bank lines") corroborates the general phenomenon
anecdotally/qualitatively (builder interviews describing crews not showing up after raids), but
there is no independent, quantified measurement of the scale of the effect nationally. NAHB itself
attributes part of construction job openings growth in JOLTS coverage to workers "without proper
documentation" leaving jobsites — a plausible but not rigorously isolated causal claim, since
several factors (higher mortgage rates, financing costs, permitting) also affect construction
pace concurrently.

**Confidence:** Low — the directional claim is plausible and has qualitative support, but it is
an assertion from an interested party about a mechanism that has not been independently
quantified.

---

## Claim 2: Construction workforce shortage of "about 300,000 workers," citing unnamed
"government data"

**Verdict:** Roughly consistent with BLS JOLTS data, but the sourcing is vague and the framing
conflates two different concepts.

**Evidence:** BLS JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) reported construction job
openings of 305,000 (year-ago) rising to 326,000 as of July 2026, with an openings rate of 3.8%.
NAHB's "~300,000" figure is in the same range as this real, public BLS series — so it is not
fabricated. However: (1) NAHB does not name BLS/JOLTS specifically in the post, which is avoidable
vagueness given the data is public and attributable; (2) "job openings" (unfilled current
postings) is a different concept from a "labor shortage" (a structural gap between needed and
available workers) — using them interchangeably overstates precision. Compare: the Associated
Builders and Contractors (ABC) workforce model — a different, model-based government/industry-data
combination — estimated the industry needs 349,000 net new workers in 2026 (down from 439,000 in
2025), and NAHB's own economics team has cited BLS-based projections of ~723,000 average annual
occupational openings in construction. These larger and differently-defined figures show that
"300,000" is a reasonable but not uniquely correct summary; the specific number chosen depends on
methodology, and NAHB picked one of several plausible, lower-end formulations without disclosing
which one.

**Confidence:** Medium — the ballpark number is defensible against a real government series
(JOLTS), but "unnamed government data" is unnecessarily opaque sourcing from an organization that
could and should cite BLS/JOLTS directly, and the shortage/openings distinction is glossed over.

---

## Claim 3: Immigrant workers are "more than one-quarter" (26.3%, a 2024 record high) of the
construction workforce

**Verdict:** Accurate.

**Evidence:** Confirmed by NAHB's own Eye On Housing analysis (constructionowners.com and
Scotsman Guide both independently reported the same 26.3% figure as a record high, based on
NAHB's analysis of Census/ACS data), and by NAHB's more detailed state/trade breakdown
(nahb.org/blog/2026/04, "Which States and Construction Trades Depend the Most on Immigrant
Workers"). This figure is trade-specific ACS-derived data with a transparent methodology and is
corroborated by independent outlets restating the same underlying NAHB analysis (no independent
recomputation was found, but the ACS/Census basis is auditable and the figure is consistently
reported across multiple outlets without contradiction).

**Confidence:** High — consistent figure across multiple sources, transparent Census/ACS
methodology, no contradicting data found.

---

## Claim 4: HBI/University of Denver study (June 2025) — ~1.98-month average delay, ~19,000 fewer
single-family homes in 2024, $10.806B annual cost

**Verdict:** Accurately represented from the primary study/press release.

**Evidence:** The HBI-commissioned, University of Denver-conducted study ("The Skilled Labor
Shortage and America's Housing Crisis," lead PI Dr. Eric Holt, released June 2025 jointly with
NAHB) reports: an **unweighted average** construction-time increase of **1.98 months** due to
labor shortage; a reduction of **~19,000 single-family homes** built in 2024; and a total
aggregate economic impact of **$10.806 billion/year**, broken into $2.663B in higher carrying
costs and $8.143B tied to the 19,000 lost homes. All three figures in the claim match the primary
source's joint press release exactly (hbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Final-Joint-Press-
Release-Labor-Shortage-Study-1.pdf and the underlying study PDF).

Important caveat the NAHB post does not surface: the 1.98-month figure is explicitly an
**unweighted average**, and the source material notes smaller builders experienced even larger
delays — meaning the average is sensitive to builder-size composition of the survey sample and is
not necessarily representative of a random draw of U.S. homebuilding activity. The study also
appears to be interview/survey-based (self-reported builder data) rather than derived from
independent administrative records (e.g., permit-to-completion timelines), which is a standard
limitation of this kind of industry-commissioned survey research, though it was conducted by
outside academic researchers (Univ. of Denver) rather than by NAHB/HBI staff directly.

**Confidence:** High for figure accuracy (they match the primary source verbatim); Medium for
representativeness of the underlying study (self-reported, unweighted-average, industry-
commissioned).

## Claim 5: The nation faces "a housing shortage of about 1.2 million units"

**Verdict:** Misleadingly presented as *the* housing shortage figure — it is NAHB's own,
narrowly-scoped, low-end estimate, not a consensus or independent figure.

**Evidence:** Independent/other-institution estimates of the U.S. housing shortage cluster far
higher: Freddie Mac ~3.7 million units (2024 estimate), Zillow ~4.7 million, Up For Growth ~3.78
million (2025 report), Brookings ~5 million, McKinsey ~8 million (per Congressional Research
Service summary and multiple secondary sources). NAHB's 1.2 million figure comes from NAHB's own
methodology (Eye On Housing, "The Housing Shortage, Explained by 2024 Data"): it measures only the
gap between current metro-level vacancy rates (ACS data) and each metro's 2005–2021 historical
"normal" vacancy rate — i.e., only the vacant-unit shortfall needed to restore normal vacancy,
split roughly evenly between rental and for-sale units. By NAHB's own admission, this method
explicitly **excludes** pent-up demand from suppressed household formation and the need to replace
aging/obsolete housing stock — the two largest drivers other estimates incorporate, which is why
NAHB's figure is roughly one-third to one-sixth the size of peer estimates. Using this
narrowest-possible-scope figure, without noting it is NAHB's own estimate (not a third-party
consensus) or that it's the low outlier among named institutions, understates the scale of the
housing shortage relative to virtually every independent estimator — an odd choice for an
organization advocating for more building, but methodologically the number is internally
consistent and transparently documented by NAHB elsewhere (just not disclosed as an outlier in
this particular post).

**Confidence:** High — NAHB's own published methodology confirms this is a narrow, self-produced,
low-end estimate; multiple independent institutions (Freddie Mac, Zillow, Up For Growth, Brookings,
McKinsey) are 3-7x higher using broader scopes.

---

## Overall assessment

The two "hard" statistics with clear primary sourcing — the 26.3% immigrant workforce share and
the HBI/Univ. of Denver study's delay/production/cost figures — check out accurately against
NAHB's and HBI's own primary publications; no numbers were misquoted or exaggerated relative to
source documents. The 300,000 worker-shortage figure is a defensible but loosely-sourced
approximation of real BLS JOLTS data, presented with unnecessary vagueness ("government data")
that a reader can't directly verify without independent research. The two weakest points are:
(1) the causal claim that enforcement is measurably "discouraging" workers and "delaying"
construction is asserted, not quantified — it rests on anecdote/member reports, not data; and
(2) the 1.2 million housing-shortage figure is NAHB's own narrowest-scope, lowest-of-the-major-
estimates figure, presented as if it were the housing shortage rather than one estimate among
several that range from 1.2M to 8M depending on methodology — a reader would reasonably assume
from the phrasing that this is a settled, neutral number, when it is actually the low end of a
wide, contested range and happens to be produced by NAHB itself.

**Does the trade-association sourcing matter here?** Yes, materially, though unevenly across the
claims. NAHB has a direct policy stake in both (a) portraying immigration enforcement as
economically harmful and (b) advocating for more construction/less regulatory friction generally.
Where NAHB is citing outside, methodologically transparent, third-party-conducted research (the
Univ. of Denver study; Census/ACS-derived immigrant-share data), the figures hold up and the
association's advocacy interest did not visibly distort them. Where NAHB is presenting its *own*
in-house estimates with real degrees of methodological freedom — the housing-shortage number in
particular, and to a lesser extent the unsourced "300,000" figure — the choices skew toward
supporting the narrative (here, curiously, toward understating the shortage's scale, since the
policy ask is about worker access rather than the shortage's size per se, and a smaller committed
"shortage" number may read as more crisis-adjacent/urgent alongside the labor point, or may simply
be the number NAHB's economics team has settled on for other purposes and is reusing here without
recontextualizing it as an outlier estimate). A careful reader should treat the workforce
percentage and the HBI study's core figures as reliable, but treat the causal
enforcement-to-delay link as an asserted industry narrative and the 1.2 million housing-shortage
figure as NAHB's own low-end, non-consensus estimate that should be read alongside — not in place
of — the 3.7-8 million range published by Freddie Mac, Zillow, Up For Growth, Brookings, and
McKinsey.
