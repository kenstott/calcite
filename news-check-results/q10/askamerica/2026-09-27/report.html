CapitolExposed's Week-37 congressional trading roundup checks out on the numbers but omits that its top two filers were separately reported as late on STOCK Act disclosures

Fact-check of capitolexposed.com/news/weekly-roundup-2026-w37, published for the disclosure window Aug 31-Sep 7, 2026

Summary

Verdict: 1 Pinocchio. Every individually checkable fact in CapitolExposed's Week-37 roundup holds up: all 10 members named are correctly identified by party, state, chamber and district (verified against Congress.gov data); Steve Cohen's Treasury-bill trade is strongly corroborated independently down to the date and dollar band; and the piece's own methodological caveat — that STOCK Act filings report a band, not an exact figure — is itself accurate. But the article's closing framing of filing timing as routine ('a filing pattern rather than a quiet market') omits that contemporaneous independent reporting (NOTUS, published the day after this window closed) found its own top two filers — Gilbert Cisneros and Kevin Hern, credited here with 66 and 29 trades respectively — were separately reported as late or non-compliant with the very 45-day STOCK Act deadline the article describes. That is a real, if modest, omission: the piece is factually sound but incomplete in a way that could leave a reader with a rosier picture of disclosure compliance than the record supports.

Claim-by-claim verdicts

🤥○○○ 1 of 4 Pinocchios

Every checkable individual fact in the piece holds up: all 10 members' names, parties, states, chambers and districts are exactly correct against Congress.gov data; the Cohen Treasury-bill trade, its date, and its 'top of band, not exact figure' framing are strongly corroborated via House-Clerk-sourced reporting and a directly fetched PTR filing; the 120-trade arithmetic is internally consistent; and the relative filer ranking (Cisneros far ahead, then Hern) is corroborated directionally by outside reporting. The one Pinocchio is for omission, not inaccuracy: the article's 'About this window' section frames the STOCK Act's 45-day deadline and this week's filing cadence as routine, but contemporaneous NOTUS reporting (published the day after this window closed) found that Cisneros and Hern -- the two filers this very article credits with the most trades of the week, 66 and 29 respectively -- were separately flagged as late or non-compliant with that same 45-day deadline. A reader of this piece alone would have no idea that its two most prominent names in the 'who filed the most' list were, per outside reporting, the subject of live disclosure-law compliance questions. That is a defensible, not egregious, omission (the proprietary conflict-alert scoring and disclosure timing are legitimately separate questions), which is why this lands at 1 rather than 2 Pinocchios.

What AskAmerica's data could and couldn't check

AskAmerica's government-data corpus has no table for STOCK Act periodic transaction reports (congressional stock trades) — search_catalog was run for both 'congressional stock trading disclosure STOCK Act' and 'member of congress financial disclosure trades periodic transaction report' and returned no matching table. This is a genuine coverage gap, not a search failure: the closest tables (SEC insider transactions, FEC contributions, lobbying filings) cover adjacent but distinct disclosure regimes.

What the corpus could check, and checked exactly right, is member identity: officials.current_members (sourced from Congress.gov) confirmed all 10 named members' party, state, chamber and district precisely as the article states — including less-obvious ones like Cleo Fields (D-LA-6) and David J. Taylor (R-OH-2), both correctly identified.

For the trading data itself — trade counts, dollar amounts, dates — verification relied on independent primary sources: a directly fetched House Clerk PTR PDF, Investing.com's House-Clerk-sourced trade summaries, and NOTUS's original reporting on STOCK Act compliance for this same period.

  • 'About this window' framing of filing lag as routine: Materially incomplete. NOTUS reported, one day after this window closed, that Cisneros was 'days or weeks late' on more than 50 trades and that Hern 'was also late, although his office denies it' — the two filers this very article lists as the week's most active. The roundup makes no mention of either being flagged for non-compliance.

What would make this worse (and why it isn't a higher rating)

This would rate 2+ Pinocchios if any of the article's own numbers were wrong, or if it affirmatively claimed compliance ('all filings were timely') rather than simply not raising the question. It doesn't do either — it's silent on timeliness beyond describing the general 45-day rule. The gap here is a professional fact-checker's kind of catch: a roundup that names its two busiest filers should, at minimum, note when those same filers are independently reported as late on the disclosure law the whole roundup exists to track. That's an editorial completeness issue, not a factual error, which is why it lands at 1 rather than 2 Pinocchios.
